Summary

This thesis addresses the design, development, and validation of a real-time human action recognition (HAR) pipeline for CCTV applications, carried out in collaboration with Innovasur. The system is based on skeleton sequences extracted from video streams, which are processed using a lightweight spatio–temporal Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) to classify predefined actions, with a special focus on detecting critical events such as falls.

To support robust training and evaluation, two complementary datasets were constructed: Le2iActions, providing initial samples of the target actions, and CCTVActions, an ad-hoc dataset tailored to real surveillance conditions. Specific data transformations and augmentations were applied to mitigate scarcity, handle occlusions, and reflect deployment scenarios. In addition, novel representation strategies were introduced, including the geometric bone encoding, which reduces variability by normalizing scale and position while emphasizing kinematic orientations, and Structured Joint Dropout, which improves robustness by simulating missing keypoints.

The classifier development followed a systematic study of training strategies, dataset combinations, and architectural choices. Experiments confirmed that lightweight GCNs, particularly CTR-GCN with 1.4M parameters, achieve reliable performance when provided with curated skeleton sequences. The full end-to-end pipeline integrates multi-person detection, 2D pose estimation, tracking, classification, and post-processing into NVIDIA DeepStream, enabling real-time inference on edge devices such as the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin.

Evaluation on the CCTVActions test set demonstrated that the proposed system achieves accurate per-subject action recognition under realistic constraints, with errors primarily arising from ambiguous cases. An explainability analysis further validated that the classifier bases its decisions on logical spatio–temporal patterns. Overall, this work shows that skeleton-based HAR offers an efficient, interpretable, and privacy-preserving solution for intelligent video analytics in smart city contexts.

Introduction

Modern cities increasingly rely on CCTV networks to monitor public spaces, optimize resources, and improve response times to incidents. Yet continuous human supervision does not scale: operators face cognitive overload, heterogeneous camera layouts, and wide variations in illumination and crowd density. This motivates intelligent video analytics that can surface actionable events in real time and provide anonymized metrics and reports about what is happening in the scene. In this landscape, human action recognition (HAR) becomes central: the system targeted in this dissertation, developed in collaboration with Innovasur, aims to recognize a predefined battery of actions for each subject present in visible-spectrum video streams, and to identify critical events (e.g., falls) or generate regular reports that assist human operators in analysis and decision making.

Most modern approaches to HAR rely on deep learning. Although RGB-based models attain strong results on academic benchmarks, they encounter significant challenges in CCTV scenarios: (i) appearance-driven inputs introduce unnecessary variability from background, lighting, or clothing, which hinders lightweight deployments; (ii) public datasets typically offer limited coverage of fixed, CCTV-like viewpoints; and (iii) achieving real-time performance on edge devices imposes strict constraints on backbone complexity and pre-processing steps (such as optical flow computation).

This dissertation develops an end-to-end, real-time HAR pipeline for city CCTV that classifies actions per tracked person using skeleton sequences as the primary representation. Starting from RGB streams, the system detects people, estimates 2D poses on the detected crops, tracks identities to form person-specific sequences, and converts keypoints into features consumed by a lightweight spatio–temporal GCN classifier. The output is a per-subject action label together with its spatio–temporal localization (bounding-box track over frames). A post-processing stage applies confidence filtering and configurable persistence criteria to raise alarms only when consistent evidence for critical actions is observed. By focusing on kinematics rather than appearance, the approach reduces domain shift, favors interpretability, and naturally supports anonymization of reports. The full pipeline is implemented on NVIDIA DeepStream for efficient ingestion, batching, and scheduling in production. Figure 1 illustrates an example visualization of the pipeline’s output.

Objectives and contributions

The dissertation set out six main objectives, all of which were successfully achieved:
  • State of the art. Review of the relevant literature and theoretical foundations to contextualize and explain the deep learning architectures employed.
  • Comparison and selection. Analysis of different deep learning approaches, identifying those most suitable under the constraints of real-time CCTV processing.
  • Curated datasets. Construction of datasets tailored to CCTV and critical events (notably falls), by merging and adapting information from diverse sources.
  • Model adaptation. Training, fine-tuning and adjustment of state-of-the-art architectures while explicitly addressing the problem of domain shift to ensure generalization in real CCTV environments.
  • Evaluation. Definition of evaluation criteria and validation of the system under industrially realistic conditions.
  • Explainability. Application of xAI techniques to interpret the model’s behavior and support the validation of its predictions.

Beyond these objectives, the dissertation also included the following contributions:

  • Datasets as a cornerstone. Development of two complementary datasets, Le2iActions and CCTVActions, which provided a solid foundation for training and evaluation, and addressed key limitations of existing public resources.
  • Representation advances. Introduction of the geometric bone skeletal representation and Structured Joint Dropout, both of which proved effective for improving robustness, emphasizing relevant kinematic features, and mitigating data scarcity.
  • Insights on design choices. Systematic experimentation provided valuable insights into training schemes, data architectures, and representation strategies, clarifying the trade-offs that arise between accuracy, generalization, and efficiency under real-time CCTV constraints.
  • Real-time deployment. Specification and integration of the complete pipeline into NVIDIA DeepStream, ensuring seamless execution of all modules (detection, pose estimation, tracking, classification, and post-processing) in a unified workflow. The system provides per-subject spatio–temporal localization together with action predictions, and includes features such as configurable persistence criteria to trigger alarms reliably. Finally, the pipeline was subjected to an extensive operational evaluation, demonstrating its robustness and viability within industrial deployment settings.

Background

This chapter outlines the fundamental concepts and techniques that form the basis of the work presented in this thesis. The first three sections follow the structure of the recent book by C. M. Bishop [1], which is used as a primary reference. We then turn to the state of the art in human action recognition, before concluding with key principles and mechanisms related to explainable AI. To set the stage, we begin with a brief introduction to neural networks, tracing their historical development and core principles.

1.1 Introduction to Neural Networks

Throughout history, humans have always sought to extract regularities from data and exploit those patterns for prediction and decision-making. What once relied on expert-crafted rules or hand-derived formulas has progressively evolved through statistics and algorithmic models. Neural networks embody a different promise: to learn the rules directly from data by composing simple, trainable building blocks into powerful, non-linear function approximators.

At a high level, a neuron computes an affine transformation followed by a non-linearity:

$$ a = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b,\qquad y = f(a), $$
where $f$ is an activation function (e.g., sigmoid, $\tanh$, ReLU). Stacking such units into layers (with hidden layers between inputs and outputs) yields a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). Non-linearity is essential: without $f$, any number of layers collapses to a single linear map. With $f$, MLPs satisfy universal approximation: for suitable $f$, a one-hidden-layer network can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to arbitrary accuracy [2] [3] [4]. See Figure 2 for an illustration of the MLP architecture.

Simple sketch of a multilayer perceptron (MLP) architecture, showing input nodes, two hidden layers, and output nodes. Obtained from [5].

For such networks, learning proceeds by minimizing a loss (or error) function, such as mean-squared error or the cross-entropy for $K$-class classification,

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} \;=\; -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^K t_{nk}\,\log p_{nk}, $$
where $t_{nk}$ is a one-hot target and $p_{nk}$ the model’s predicted probability (e.g., softmax output). Beyond fitting the training set (empirical risk), we care about generalization, that is, low expected loss on new samples from the data distribution. Strategies such as regularization (e.g., weight decay), cross-validation, dropout, early stopping, and appropriate inductive biases (i.e., assumptions built into a model’s design that guide how it learns from limited data) help bridge this gap.

The historical development of neural networks is often framed around three major waves: the initial perceptron, the introduction of backpropagation, and the advent of deep learning.

1.1.1 The Perceptron

The first wave of neural network research was pioneered through the perceptron, originally inspired by the human brain. This single-layer classifier applies a step activation $f(a)=\mathbb{I}[a>0]$ to separate classes with a hyperplane. Its learning rule provably converges in finitely many steps when the data are linearly separable, as shown in the original paper from Rosenblatt in 1958 [6]. Early hardware prototypes showcased automatic weight adjustment.

However, the model's linear separability limitation (e.g., failure on XOR) was rigorously analyzed by Minsky and Papert in [7]. The perception that such limits extended to multi-layer networks, together with the absence of a practical training method for them, fueled a decline in funding and interest, leading to the first AI winter. The core lesson endures: linear models are fast and interpretable, but non-linearity and depth are needed for producing complex decision boundaries.

1.1.2 Backpropagation

The renaissance arrived with the standardization of error backpropagation in 1986 [8], which provided an efficient way to compute gradients of the loss $\mathcal{L}$ via the chain rule and to update parameters using (stochastic) gradient descent. In its simplest form, the gradient with respect to the weights of layer $\ell$ can be written as
$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(\ell)}} \;=\; \delta^{(\ell)} \, (h^{(\ell-1)})^\top, \qquad \delta^{(\ell)} \;=\; (W^{(\ell+1)})^\top \delta^{(\ell+1)} \, f'(a^{(\ell)}), $$
where $\delta^{(\ell)}$ denotes the error propagated to layer $\ell$, $h^{(\ell-1)}$ are the activations of the previous layer, and $f'(a^{(\ell)})$ is the derivative of the activation function. In essence, backpropagation propagates error signals backward from the output layer to the input, allowing all weights in the network to be adjusted.

Choosing differentiable activations (sigmoid, $\tanh$, later ReLU) and losses (such as cross entropy) made multi-layer training practical. In parallel, a probabilistic perspective clarified that, with a softmax output and cross-entropy loss, the network learns to approximate posterior class probabilities, thus connecting neural networks with statistical inference [9]. Despite these advances, deeper MLPs remained difficult to optimize and often relied on engineered features, a sign of an impending bottleneck and, for a time, waning enthusiasm relative to kernel methods and SVMs.

1.1.3 Deep learning

What changed in the 2010s was scale (data, models, compute) and architecture. Large labeled datasets, GPU acceleration, and novel architectural design choices (e.g., convolutions for images, later residual connections to ease optimization) enabled training of deep networks with dozens or hundreds of layers. Depth brings powerful representation learning: each layer transforms raw inputs into more abstract features, letting the final classifier operate in a linearly more separable space. Empirically, deeper non-linear compositions often generalize better when trained with appropriate regularization and data augmentation. This deep learning phase overcame earlier stagnation, delivered breakthroughs across perception and language, and reframed generalization as the interplay of optimization dynamics and data scale. Importantly, the universal approximation insight was not the end but the beginning: depth governs how efficiently functions are approximated, making non-linear deep architectures central to modern machine learning.

At this stage, with deep learning widely adopted, several natural questions arise: to what extent can complex phenomena be captured from data alone, and when is the incorporation of explicit structure (such as symmetries, dynamics, or causality) beneficial? The "Bitter Lesson" [10] suggests that, historically, general methods that exploit computation and data have consistently prevailed over approaches relying heavily on human-specified structure. Even so, the answers are likely problem-dependent, and ongoing research continues to elucidate how data, objectives, and inductive biases interact, leaving open the possibility of further principled advances.

1.2 Convolutional Neural Networks

Having introduced neural networks in general, we now turn to a design that aligns the model with the geometry of images and other grid-structured signals. Fully connected layers treat inputs as unordered vectors, but pixels are arranged on a lattice with strong local correlations. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) build this structure into the architecture: small filters are reused across the image to capture recurring local patterns while keeping the number of parameters manageable. Together with non-linearities and spatial downsampling, this makes learning directly from raw pixels practical and stable.

1.2.1 Convolution Operation

The core operation is a discrete convolution between a small learnable kernel $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ (a tiny matrix of weights) and an input image $X$ (a 2D array of pixels). At each spatial location $(i,j)$ the computation is

$$ z_{i,j} \;=\; \sum_{u=1}^{m} \sum_{v=1}^{n} W_{u,v}\, X_{i+u-1,\;j+v-1} \;+\; b, $$
which produces a feature map $Z=(z_{i,j})$ highlighting where the pattern encoded by $W$ appears in $X$. A pointwise non-linearity (e.g., ReLU) is then applied to $Z$. In Figure 3 we illustrate this operation.

In practice, each layer learns a bank of filters (several kernels), yielding multiple feature maps that are stacked into channels. If the input has multiple channels (e.g., RGB), each kernel extends across them and the sum in (Equation 4) also aggregates over channels. Two hyperparameters control the geometry: the stride $s$ (how many pixels the kernel moves at each step) and the padding $p$ (zeros added around the border to control output size and avoid shrinking). Spatial resolution can be reduced either by using stride $s$ or by pooling (e.g., max/average over a small window), which makes the representation less sensitive to small shifts and enlarges the receptive field (the region of the input that influences a given activation).

1.2.2 Features and inductive bias

Two architectural choices give CNNs their advantage on images. First, local connectivity: filters look at small neighborhoods, reflecting the fact that nearby pixels are more related than distant ones. Second, weight sharing: the same filter is applied at every location, drastically reducing parameters and yielding approximate translation equivariance (a shifted input produces a shifted response).

When combined with pooling or striding, deeper layers develop a degree of translation invariance. Stacking these operations across layers produces hierarchical features: early filters resemble edge or texture detectors, intermediate layers respond to parts and motifs, and deeper layers capture object-level patterns. The result is a representation in which a simple linear head can often separate classes effectively.

1.2.3 Impact and limitations

Historically, LeNet-5 demonstrated end-to-end learning from pixels for handwritten digits [12], and the ImageNet breakthrough of AlexNet established deep CNNs as standard backbones in modern vision [13]. Today, such backbones underpin practical pipelines central to this work: person detection (e.g., region-based detectors that localize humans in the scene) and human pose estimation (predicting joint heatmaps or keypoints) [14] [15] [16]. We will discuss some of these architectures in Section 2.5.

Despite their strengths, CNNs assume a regular grid and fixed spatial locality; they are not inherently invariant to rotation or scale and typically rely on data augmentation or specialized modules to gain robustness. Capturing very long-range dependencies may also be challenging with purely local filters. These considerations motivate complementary mechanisms, such as attention [17], and models that operate beyond regular grids, such as Graph Neural Networks for skeletons and other relational structures (see Section 1.3).

1.2.4 Spatio-Temporal Convolutions

While 2D CNNs operate on individual frames, many visual tasks involve temporal dynamics across consecutive frames. 3D Convolutional Neural Networks (3D-CNNs) extend the convolution operator by adding a time dimension, with kernels $W \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times m \times n}$ spanning multiple frames to jointly capture appearance and motion. This enables recognition not only of what appears in the image, but also how it evolves over time.

The key advantage of 3D-CNNs is their end-to-end ability to learn motion cues directly, without relying on external features such as optical flow. Their main limitation is the high computational cost and the need for large-scale datasets to avoid overfitting, yet they remain central to modern video understanding [18] [19].

1.3 Graph Neural Networks

Many data domains are naturally graph-structured rather than grid-like. Social networks, molecules, knowledge graphs, point clouds, and articulated human poses can all be represented as graphs. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) extend neural modeling to these cases by replacing convolutions on regular grids with message passing along graph edges. Each node updates its representation by aggregating information from its neighbors, providing an inductive bias to locality now defined by the graph connectivity [20].

1.3.1 From convolution to message passing

In a 2D grid, convolution mixes information from a fixed local patch. In a graph, neighborhoods are irregular: each node may have a variable degree and no natural ordering. The core operation in modern GNNs is the graph convolution, often written as
$$ h_v^{(k)} = \sigma \Big( W^{(k)} \cdot \text{AGG}\big(h_v^{(k-1)}, \{\,h_u^{(k-1)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}\big) \Big), $$
where $h_v^{(0)} = x_v$ are the input features of node $v$, $\text{AGG}(\cdot)$ is a permutation-invariant aggregation over its neighbors (mean, sum, or attention-weighted), $W^{(k)}$ are trainable weights, and $\sigma$ is a nonlinear activation. After $K$ layers, each node embedding $h_v^{(K)}$ summarizes information from its $K$-hop neighborhood. GNNs using this mechanism are sometimes referred to as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs). 1.3.2 Perspectives and design choices

Two main perspectives coexist. Spectral methods define filters in the Laplacian eigenbasis, which is mathematically elegant but computationally demanding and graph-specific [21] [22]. Spatial methods, in contrast, perform local neighbor aggregation directly as in Eq. (Equation 5), scaling linearly with the number of edges and adapting easily to unseen graphs. Most practical models adopt the spatial view.

Typical design choices include the type of aggregation (sum, mean, max, or attention), the definition of the adjacency (fixed, partitioned, or partially learned), and the network depth. Deeper models enlarge the receptive field but suffer from over-smoothing, where node features become indistinguishable; thus, most effective GNNs employ only a few layers and rely on residual connections or normalization to preserve expressiveness. For graph-level tasks, a readout operation aggregates node embeddings into a single representation for classification, as illustrated in Figure 5.

1.3.3 Strengths and challenges

GNNs combine node attributes with structural information, handle graphs of varying size and topology, and generalize inductively to unseen subgraphs. Their computational cost is roughly linear in the number of edges, making them efficient for sparse, real-world graphs.

However, several challenges remain: deep GNNs are prone to over-smoothing, scaling to very large or dense graphs often requires sampling or clustering, and modeling heterogeneous or dynamic graphs demands specialized extensions.

1.3.4 Temporal extensions

When node features evolve over time, the graph becomes dynamic. Spatio-temporal GNNs (ST-GNNs) extend the framework by combining spatial graph convolutions with temporal models such as 1D convolutions, recurrent networks, or attention [23] [24]. Conceptually, this is equivalent to adding temporal edges that connect each node to itself across consecutive frames. Such architectures have proven effective in diverse tasks, from traffic forecasting to human action recognition, where a skeleton sequence is represented as a series of graphs of joints connected by anatomical edges. Some of these models are examined in Subsection 2.5.5.

1.4 State of the Art in Human Action Recognition

In this section, we review the state of the art in video-based human action recognition (HAR), focusing on approaches relevant to real-world (“in the wild”) settings and applications such as fall detection. Our discussion builds upon prior surveys [25] [26] [27] [28], which primarily focus on neural network–based methods, underscoring their dominance over handcrafted features and classical machine learning approaches. Modern HAR methods can be grouped into four broad categories:

  1. 2D CNN-based methods operating on RGB frames (often with optical flow in a two-stream architecture),
  2. 3D CNN-based and hybrid architectures that learn spatio-temporal features directly or via recurrent networks,
  3. Transformer-based video models leveraging self-attention mechanisms, and
  4. Skeleton-based methods that use human pose sequences (with graph or recurrent models).

We outline each category below, highlighting how they handle spatial and temporal information, key contributions, and typical trade-offs in complexity and data requirements.

In Section 2.2 and Section 2.5, we provide perspectives and motivations for adopting skeleton-based representations as the foundation of our real-time CCTV HAR approach, while the specific design decisions for classifier development are presented in Chapter 3. An overview of existing datasets for our task, along with their limitations, is provided in Subsection 2.3.2.

1.4.1 2D CNN-Based Methods

1.4.1.1 Spatial CNNs with Optical Flow

Early deep learning approaches to HAR applied 2D convolutional neural networks to video frames, treating action recognition as an extension of image classification. A prime example is the two-stream CNN architecture introduced by Simonyan and Zisserman [29]. Their model consists of a spatial stream (a CNN processing individual RGB frames to capture appearance information) and a temporal stream (a CNN processing stacked optical flow fields to capture motion between frames). The two streams' outputs are fused (e.g., via score averaging or SVM-based fusion) to produce the final action prediction.

This design explicitly separates spatial features (objects, scenes in each frame) from temporal motion cues (frame-to-frame movement), leveraging the strength of pre-trained 2D CNNs for the spatial part. The temporal stream, fed with dense optical flow, learns to recognize patterns of motion; notably, [29] showed that a ConvNet trained on multi-frame optical flow can yield competitive accuracy even with limited video training data.

Two-stream models achieved strong results on video benchmarks like UCF-101 [30] and HMDB-51 [31], in part because the optical flow input helps focus on human motion while being invariant to background appearance.

1.4.1.2 Extensions and Trade-offs
Subsequent works built on the two-stream idea to better capture longer temporal structure. For instance, the Temporal Segment Network (TSN) framework [32] samples frames from multiple temporal segments of a video, processes each with a 2D CNN (spatial and motion streams), and then aggregates the predictions.

Generally, 2D CNN-based methods are computationally efficient during training since they can use ImageNet -pretrained models for the spatial stream and treat each frame (or short snippet) independently. However, a key limitation for real-time usage is the reliance on external optical flow computation and relatively simple temporal fusion. Computing dense optical flow for every video is costly, and the model only captures short-term motion unless additional temporal modeling (like segment sampling or an RNN) is added.

In unconstrained scenarios, 2D CNN with optical flow approaches remain competitive due to their robustness to background clutter (the motion stream ignores static backgrounds) and modest data requirements. Yet, their limited temporal modeling capacity means they might miss complex or long-duration actions. For specific applications like fall detection, two-stream CNNs can be effective if the fall exhibits distinctive motion patterns, but they may need careful tuning, for instance, ensuring that optical flow emphasizes rapid posture changes while suppressing camera-induced motion.

1.4.2 3D CNN-Based and Hybrid Architectures

1.4.2.1 3D Convolutional Networks

As already described in Section 1.2, 3D-CNNs learn spatio-temporal features by convolving across both spatial dimensions and time, enabling motion to be captured directly as part of the representation rather than relying on optical flow. Tran et al. introduced this idea with the C3D network, showing that 3D ConvNets can simultaneously model appearance and short-term motion, outperforming 2D ConvNets on action recognition tasks [18].

Carreira and Zisserman later proposed the I3D architecture, which “inflates” 2D Inception filters into 3D and leverages ImageNet -pretrained weights to initialize the spatial filters [19]. Combined with large-scale training on Kinetics [33], I3D achieved then state-of-the-art performance by jointly learning from RGB and optical flow streams. More recently, SlowFast networks improved efficiency and temporal modeling by combining a slow pathway (low frame rate, detailed semantics) with a fast pathway (high frame rate, rapid motion), fused through lateral connections [34].

1.4.2.2 Hybrid CNN-RNN Models
Another way to handle temporal data is to combine 2D CNNs for spatial feature extraction with recurrent neural networks for temporal modeling. In these hybrid architectures, a CNN (often pretrained) processes each frame or short clip to produce a feature vector, and then a sequence model (like an LSTM or GRU) processes the feature sequence to recognize the action. For example, the LRCN model by Donahue et al. [35] used a CNN (for each frame) whose output features are fed into an LSTM that learns the temporal dynamics of the action.

The trade-offs between 3D CNNs and CNN+RNN hybrids include:

  • 3D CNNs tend to have a large number of parameters and require substantial training data (e.g., Kinetics [33]) to learn effective spatio-temporal kernels, but once trained or pre-trained they offer an end-to-end solution.
  • CNN-RNN hybrids can be easier to train on smaller datasets by splitting the problem (pretrain CNN on images, then train RNN on video sequences). They are better suited for modeling variable-length actions, but their two-stage nature may not capture fine-grained motion as effectively as 3D convolutions.

In practice, 3D CNNs have emerged as the flagship models for video recognition on large-scale benchmarks, whereas hybrid approaches have proved effective in data-limited scenarios or when leveraging pretrained image models through transfer learning.

1.4.3 Transformer-Based Video Models

1.4.3.1 Self-Attention for Space-Time

Inspired by the success of Transformers in NLP and image recognition, recent HAR models apply self-attention mechanisms to video. A representative model is TimeSformer by Bertasius et al. [36], which factorizes the attention along spatial and temporal dimensions. Another is ViViT (Video Vision Transformer) by Arnab et al. [37], which explores different architectures for applying a pure Transformer to video clips.

These transformer models have achieved state-of-the-art results on large video benchmarks. They excel at capturing global context, relating the beginning of an action to its end even if many frames apart.

1.4.3.2 Capabilities and Trade-offs

The strength of transformers in HAR comes at a cost:

  • Pure self-attention models typically require extensive training data and compute.
  • Without the inductive bias of convolution, transformers can overfit smaller datasets.
  • Computational complexity grows quadratically with the number of patches/frames.

Efficient attention variants and hybrid models (e.g., Video Swin Transformer [38]) address this, striking a balance between scalability and accuracy and making transformer-based HAR more practical in real-world settings.

1.4.4 Skeleton-Based Methods

This approach focuses on the skeleton representation of the human body, obtained by estimating the 2D or 3D positions of key joints. The video is thus converted into a sequence of pose vectors or graphs, removing background information and highlighting human motion explicitly.

1.4.4.1 RNN/GCN Models

Early deep learning works on skeleton-based action recognition employed recurrent neural networks: for instance, Du et al. used a hierarchical RNN to model human joint coordinates over time [39].

On the other hand, Yan et al. introduced the Spatial-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) [24], which constructs a graph where nodes are fixed body joints and edges represent both anatomical and temporal links.

A notable refinement of this approach is the Two-Stream Adaptive GCN (2s-AGCN) by Shi et al. [40], which learns adaptive adjacency and adds an edge stream. More recently, Liu et al. proposed ProtoGCN [41], which leverages prototype learning to enhance fine-grained motion patterns and better distinguish similar actions.

1.4.4.2 Fall Detection and HAR in the Wild
Skeleton-based methods are particularly promising for fall detection (and HAR), since they are invariant to background clutter and focus directly on human kinematics. Chen et al. [42] demonstrate this by leveraging pose sequences and temporal convolutions, achieving $99.8$% accuracy on the fall class of NTU RGB+D [43] in real time. Similarly, other approaches confirm that relying on skeletons alleviates sensitivity to lighting or scene appearance, which is a common limitation in RGB-based methods [44]. Additional advantages of this approach are presented in Section 2.2.

Recent advances in pose estimation further reinforce this trend. For instance, lightweight real-time frameworks based on modified YOLO [14] enhance the robustness of multi-person pose extraction under occlusions and scale variations [45], which directly benefits downstream HAR tasks such as fall detection. These results highlight how pose-driven representations provide a robust foundation for recognizing human actions in unconstrained environments.

1.5 Explainable AI

The use of artificial intelligence in increasingly critical contexts requires the integration of explainable AI (xAI) techniques that can unravel the functioning of models as robustly as possible. Such methods should enable us to understand which aspects of the input led the model to a specific prediction, thereby supporting methodological decision-making regarding both models and datasets, while also satisfying reliability and safety standards that are essential in high-responsibility settings and are now a growing priority for institutions [46]. In particular, Innovasur was recently certified under the ISO/IEC 42001:2023 standard [47].

To interpret the complex deep models employed in this work, we rely on post-hoc explainability methods [48] [49] rather than intrinsically interpretable models. Such models (e.g., small decision trees or linear models) are transparent by design but often sacrifice accuracy in complex tasks such as human activity recognition (HAR). In contrast, post-hoc methods allow us to use high-performing models (e.g., deep neural networks) and then analyze their decisions after training. This approach preserves predictive power while still yielding human-understandable explanations of model behavior.

We specifically focus on feature attribution [50] [51] techniques that explain individual predictions by attributing credit or blame to input features. Other strategies (such as approximating the model with a simpler surrogate model [52] or identifying influential training examples [53]) exist, but feature attributions provide a direct way to ask why a particular prediction was made for a given input.

1.5.1 Integrated Gradients

The Integrated Gradients (IG) [50] post-hoc technique attributes a model output $F(x)$ to each input feature by integrating the gradient of $F$ along a straight path from a baseline $x'$ to the input $x$. For feature $i$,

$$ \mathrm{IG}_i(x;x') \;=\; (x_i - x'_i)\; \int_{0}^{1} \frac{\partial F\!\big(x' + \alpha\,(x - x')\big)}{\partial x_i}\, d\alpha~, $$
where $F:\mathbb{R}^n\!\to\!\mathbb{R}$ is the (differentiable) scoring function, returning a scalar output (e.g., a logit value or the probability of a given class in classification tasks). In practice, the integral is approximated with a Riemann sum over $m$ steps:
$$ \mathrm{IG}_i(x;x') \;\approx\; (x_i - x'_i)\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{\partial F\!\big(x' + \tfrac{k}{m}(x-x')\big)}{\partial x_i}\, . $$

This approximation amounts to computing gradients in a simple loop, an efficient operation that is readily supported by modern deep learning frameworks.

On the baseline $x'$

The baseline represents a reference input against which attributions are measured (a proxy for absence of signal). Typical choices include a zero/neutral input, a blurred or averaged sample, or a class-conditional reference; the choice should be meaningful for the modality and task so that the straight path $x'\!\to\!x$ traverses semantically plausible states. Different baselines emphasize distinct counterfactual questions (e.g., "what changes relative to no signal?" vs. "what distinguishes this class from a typical other class?").

Axioms and properties

IG was designed to satisfy key desirable axioms ensuring faithful, implementation-agnostic attributions:
  • Completeness. The attributions account for the model output difference between input and baseline (up to the integration step approximation):
    $$ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{IG}_i(x;x') \;=\; F(x) - F(x')~. $$
  • Sensitivity. If $x$ and $x'$ differ only in feature $i$ and $F(x)\neq F(x')$, then that feature must receive non-zero attribution, as it is solely responsible for the change:
    $$ \big(\forall j\!\neq\! i:\; x_j=x'_j\big)\ \wedge\ \big(F(x)\neq F(x')\big) \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{IG}_i(x;x') \neq 0~. $$
  • Implementation Invariance. If two networks $F$ and $G$ are functionally equivalent ($F(z)=G(z)$ for all $z$), then their IG attributions coincide for any $(x,x')$:
    $$ F\equiv G \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{IG}^{F}(x;x') \;=\; \mathrm{IG}^{G}(x;x')~. $$

These guarantees, together with the simple path-integration formulation, make IG broadly applicable to any differentiable model without architectural changes. More generally, it has been shown that path-based attribution methods are the only ones that can simultaneously satisfy these axioms. This axiomatic grounding is crucial: it ensures that attribution maps reflect the true behavior of the model, so that any unexpected or undesirable pattern can be safely attributed to the model itself, rather than to limitations of the explanation method.

Many attribution methods violate these principles. For example, classical saliency maps [54] rely on local gradients and often suffer from gradient saturation, producing near-zero attributions even when the model output changes significantly. Similarly, Guided Backpropagation [55] violates Sensitivity by propagating gradients only through active ReLUs, while DeepLIFT [56] breaks Implementation Invariance by relying on discrete gradient approximations. IG overcomes these limitations by integrating gradients along the baseline-to-input path, resulting in more faithful and class-discriminative attributions, as illustrated in Figure 6.

1.5.2 Expected Gradients

A main limitation of the Integrated Gradients method is its reliance on a single baseline $x'$ and a single straight integration path $\gamma(\alpha) = x' + \alpha(x-x')$. This makes IG highly sensitive to the specific choice of $x'$ and less robust when there is no clear or semantically meaningful reference input.

The Expected Gradients (EG) technique [57] addresses this issue by averaging Integrated Gradients over a distribution of baselines $\mathcal{D}$ and random interpolation factors $\alpha \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$:

$$ \mathrm{EG}(x) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x' \sim \mathcal{D},\;\alpha \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)} \Big[ (x - x') \odot \nabla_x F\big(x' + \alpha (x - x')\big) \Big]. $$
This probabilistic formulation is equivalent to a double integral over all possible baselines and interpolation points, and therefore retains all the desirable properties of IG (completeness, sensitivity, and implementation invariance), while integrating over many plausible counterfactuals rather than relying on a single baseline. This significantly reduces variance and improves the robustness of the resulting attributions.

Note that this approach is not equivalent to computing IG from a mean baseline $\bar{x'}=\mathbb{E}[x']$; EG averages IG over many baselines and interpolation points, and these operations do not commute because IG depends on $F$ along the baseline–input path. Unless $F$ is affine, which is not the case for deep neural networks,

$$ \mathbb{E}_{x'}[\mathrm{IG}(x;x')] \neq \mathrm{IG}\,\!\big(x;\mathbb{E}[x']\big). $$

In practice, EG is applied following existing implementations [58] that approximate this expectation via Monte Carlo for computational efficiency: draw multiple baselines $x' \!\sim\! \mathcal{D}$ from a background set (e.g., a representative subset of the training set), sample interpolation factors $\alpha \!\sim\! \mathcal{U}(0,1)$ along each baseline–input path, evaluate the gradients, and average the resulting attributions. Moreover, EG can be viewed as a gradient-based approximation of SHAP values [59], which are the unique additive feature attributions that satisfy properties of local accuracy, missingness, and consistency.

1.5.3 CAM, SHAP and Beyond

Beyond feature-attribution techniques such as IG and EG, two post-hoc families are widely used in vision-based HAR (video or pose/skeleton pipelines): Class Activation Mapping (CAM/Grad-CAM) and SHAP-based methods.

CAM/Grad-CAM
CAM [60] and Grad-CAM [61] generate coarse, spatially oriented maps over pixels or regions. When applied to temporal data such as joints or frame sequences, they remain computationally efficient. In HAR settings, both methods often produce almost identical rankings and exhibit high stability under small, kinematically plausible perturbations, although faithfulness may vary across classes [62].
SHAP-based methods
In practice, Kernel SHAP and Deep SHAP are employed within the SHAP framework [59] to generate instance-level and feature-level attributions. A direct comparison in HAR with GCN backbones shows that SHAP-based methods capture fine-grained feature contributions, whereas Grad-CAM provides faster and more region-focused explanations. These approaches are best regarded as complementary, depending on whether precise feature attribution or spatial saliency is required, with additional trade-offs related to reference set choice and computational cost [63].
Additional Methods
For models operating on graph-structured data such as pose graphs, the xAI toolbox includes perturbation explainers (e.g., GNNExplainer [64], PGExplainer [65]), which highlight subgraphs or masks, and surrogate explainers (e.g., GraphLIME [66], GraphSVX [67]), which fit local interpretable models. These methods provide substructure or feature-level rationales when CAM/Grad-CAM or SHAP-based approaches prove insufficient [68].

Methodology

To build a curated deep learning–based system capable of reporting human actions in a CCTV scene in real time, we should follow a series of well-defined steps. First, we must precisely define the actions to be modelled. Next, we determine how the data representing these actions will be encoded, and collect high-quality datasets tailored to that purpose. Finally, we carefully design the pipeline architecture to meet the desired objective, outlining the data preprocessing and transformation steps under consideration.

2.1 Target Actions

We first generated a very detailed report defining the seven actions of interest for this use case, including a range of examples for each one. Below is a condensed summary of that report, outlining the main criteria defined for each action:
  • Standing: Person remains upright within a very limited area, without walking or changing location. Minimal movements are allowed, such as shifting weight, rotating slightly, or leaning against a wall.
  • Walking: Person moves steadily on foot, regardless of pace or direction, including walking in corridors or across open spaces. Excludes very short or ambiguous steps (to avoid confusion with standing) and movements resembling a run.
  • Sitting: Person remains seated on a stable surface (chair, bench, step, or floor) or in a clearly low position such as crouching or kneeling. Light posture changes are allowed.
  • Falling: Uncontrolled loss of balance resulting in contact with the ground or another surface, ending in a sitting or lying position. Includes forward, backward, or lateral falls, as well as slow collapses due to dizziness. Excludes voluntary descents or destabilisations without ground contact.
  • Getting up: Transition from a low position (sitting, lying, crouching) to a more upright one, typically standing. The label covers only the upward motion.
  • Bending down: Controlled downward movement toward the ground, such as starting to sit, lie down, crouch, or kneel. Must be intentional and progressive.
  • Lying down: Person is horizontally positioned and stable (on the ground or a surface), face up, face down, or on the side. Small movements or posture adjustments are allowed.

These actions can be grouped into static and non-static classes, depending on whether they involve significant movement of body parts (walking, falling, getting up, and bending down) or not (standing, sitting, and lying down). It is worth noting that walking is the only class that may entail significant displacement from an initial position. Aside from that, we distinguish two types of actions according to their temporal nature:

  • Continuous actions: Those actions that can be sustained over a relatively long and stable period of time, including standing, walking, sitting and lying down.
  • Transitional actions: Short movements that serve as transitions between two continuous actions, including falling, getting up, and bending down. Their duration is typically less than 5 seconds.

Among the defined classes, falling is the primary target, as it represents the most critical action to detect in a surveillance context. The class lying down may sometimes serve as confirmation of a falling event, and is therefore also worth considering. Moreover, standing, walking, and sitting are typical situations in transit areas and public spaces, which are among the most common deployment scenarios. Automatically detecting these can enable periodic reporting or trigger alarms in cases of loitering or inappropriate use of a given space. While bending down and getting up may appear less relevant, their inclusion serves three main purposes:

  1. Including these two transitional actions creates a closed set of classes, leaving little room for ambiguity in the scenarios of interest. In practice, most activity between any two timestamps can be segmented into actions corresponding to these categories. Some edge cases remain, as discussed in Section 5.3, with the absence of a running class being the most notable; however, this set is adequate for a first version of a production-oriented system.
  2. Omitting these classes, particularly bending down, could cause the model to confuse them with falling, leading to false alarms in common situations. Their inclusion allows a model to learn more accurate decision boundaries, and expanding from five to seven classes does not significantly increase complexity.
  3. Although these distinctions could be incorporated into other classes (e.g., grouping bending down and getting up with sitting and ensuring balanced instances), defining them separately provides greater flexibility in post-processing predictions. Given their transitional nature, they are likely to connect only limited sets of actions. For example, following a persistent and confident bending down prediction, only sitting, lying down, or getting up should follow in most cases, whereas a direct transition to standing is not possible. Such constraints reduce the space of possible predictions, occasionally forcing the model to override its initial (and possibly incorrect) choice. This enables the application of purely logical rules, or monotonic constraints, that are always satisfied in practice.

Thus, our collection of actions is clearly specified, allowing us to establish precise guidelines for the subsequent data acquisition process.

2.2 Data Representation

In the context of action classification, the data can be formalized as pairs $(x,y)$ where $x$ is an instance and $y$ is one of the predefined action classes. The first design choice is how instances $x$ are represented.

Human actions are to be classified from RGB video streams. However, a single frame is insufficient to reliably determine the ongoing activity, so multiple time-ordered frames must be considered.

In other words, the instances need to be spatio-temporal, effectively represented in the form of video clips. Ideally, a certain clip should capture only a single action of interest throughout its duration, ensuring that no other actions occur and avoiding label ambiguity within the sample.

The objective is to identify actions at the level of individual people rather than at the scene level. Consequently, predictions should be associated with specific tracked identities. Assuming a person detector, a tracker, and a classifier capable of per-identity predictions, we could model each instance as a sequence of RGB crops centered on a single person.

However, this crop-based representation has some limitations:

  • Appearance cues are often unnecessary, and while background context may sometimes assist recognition, it is not strictly required to determine the action.
  • RGB inputs exhibit high intra-class variability, along with the inherent noise of image data, which increases the burden on lightweight models intended for real-time use.
  • Appearance-centric approaches typically demand larger and more diverse datasets to generalize, thereby raising the risk of overfitting under limited data, increasing susceptibility to edge cases, and complicating error debugging.
  • Moreover, simple cropping around the subject removes global information about the person's position within the scene, which makes it harder to capture actions strongly tied to absolute motion patterns, such as walking.

We therefore adopt a simplified and more structured representation of action instances based on human skeletons (also referred to as human poses, landmarks, body joints, or keypoints) extracted from RGB frames, as supported in prior works [69] [70] [71] [24]. A human skeleton corresponding to a single subject in a certain frame can be encoded as an undirected graph, where each node corresponds to a body joint, and spatial edges connect these joints according to natural connectivity maps of the human body structure. Typically, nodes contain three or four values representing the 2D or 3D coordinates of each joint in a given frame, together with a confidence score that indicates the reliability of the body part being located at that position.

Different keypoint topologies can be employed to represent the human skeleton. In our case, we adopt a whole-body topology that includes all body parts required for the actions of interest. Specifically, we rely on the standard format of the COCO Keypoints dataset [72] [73], which defines 17 two-dimensional keypoints: the nose, the left and right eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles. In Figure 7, we illustrate some of the most common skeleton configurations defined in different datasets, showing the connections between keypoints. Two notes should be made regarding this choice:

  • We retain 17 keypoints since this provides a balanced set of joints, and most state-of-the-art methods readily support this format [78] [79] [80]. We acknowledge that some joints, such as facial landmarks, may be superfluous for recognizing the actions of interest, and there is evidence suggesting that fewer keypoints may suffice for certain tasks [81]. Here, however, we prioritize pipeline integration and scalability by adopting a widely used configuration.
  • We restrict ourselves to 2D coordinates, as they are generally more reliable [82]. Estimating 3D coordinates typically requires motion sensors [83], multi-view camera setups [84], or more complex pipelines that often build upon 2D keypoints [85]. Nevertheless, 3D information remains beneficial for specific applications, as it can effectively mitigate the uncertainty arising from variations in camera viewpoints and angles.

Ultimately, sequences of skeletons of different identities tracked over time constitute our data instances. These sequences can themselves be interpreted as graphs, where the temporal edges connect the same joints across time. In essence, this approach provides six main advantages:

  1. Structured nature. This representation captures correlations both spatially, by emphasizing relevant interactions among well-defined body parts, and temporally, by modeling dependencies across time steps. Exploiting these spatio-temporal relations is crucial to accurately characterize human actions.
  2. Noise reduction. Focusing on kinematic structure reduces irrelevant visual variability such as clothing, background, and lighting, partially mitigating domain shift issues and providing cleaner inputs.
  3. Privacy. Indeed, skeletons inherently anonymize individuals by discarding identifiable visual features, addressing privacy concerns in surveillance since no critical RGB data is required for training a classifier.
  4. Sample efficiency. By providing a compact, task-relevant, spatio-temporal structure, this representation lowers the data needed to learn discriminative boundaries compared to raw RGB inputs.
  5. Storage and compute. Skeleton annotations are lighter to store and process than RGB crops, which facilitates training and real-time inference.
  6. Global context. Skeletons are able to preserve the absolute position of each body joint within the scene while suppressing background clutter, providing spatial information that a classifier can exploit.

Although skeleton-based approaches to action recognition are still relatively new and remain underdeveloped, with several issues that need to be addressed (e.g., variance of instances due to translations within the scene, occlusion sensitivity, subsequent pipeline intricacy, annotation ambiguity and effort, etc), we believe they nonetheless provide a powerful representation of data.

The next step is to define a data acquisition procedure that produces samples with these properties so that a classifier can be trained effectively.

2.3 Datasets

Data is the fuel that powers all machine learning solutions. Since the emergence of deep learning, progress in this field has been driven by the availability of large-scale datasets. Both architectures and hardware have evolved to handle this ever-increasing volume of data. However, collecting high-quality data, especially video or image-based, is an extremely time-consuming process. When aiming to build state-of-the-art, real-world solutions with limited resources, it becomes essential to define strategies that make this task more manageable. This section formalizes these strategies, balancing available resources against project needs, and summarizes the key characteristics of the datasets we curated to train and tune our system.

2.3.1 Data Acquisition Strategy

To train a system with the requirements outlined in the Introduction, we need an adequate number of video sequences depicting instances of the predefined set of actions described in Section 2.1. Since our objective is to recognize actions at the level of individual people, we require these sequences to be annotated with the spatial positions of the actors over time, which is typically done in the form of bounding boxes. Moreover, as previously discussed, using skeleton-based data for both training and inference allows us to afford a reduction in the number of required samples, as it eliminates the noise inherent in RGB data and provides cleaner, more structured inputs for the model. This balance between sample size and informational value lies at the core of our methodology.

Our approach to data acquisition focuses on diversity and precision over large-scale quantity, in line with findings from works such as [86] or [87]. We confidently adopt this procedure, grounded in the premise that pretrained models on larger datasets already provide a solid knowledge base. Thus, our goal is to tailor these models to the nuances of our action categories and ensure effective domain adaptation to CCTV scenarios, by using a sufficient amount of high-quality, diverse, and representative data gathered within reasonable time constraints.

However, this approach still requires accurate keypoint annotations for every frame in each sequence, which constitute the essential information that the classifier will ultimately exploit to learn the spatio-temporal patterns of human actions. Consequently, when a dataset provides skeleton data, bounding boxes are not strictly necessary. Nevertheless, in cases where bounding boxes are available but skeletons are absent, state-of-the-art top-down pose estimators can be applied to extract skeletons from the cropped regions, typically with high accuracy provided that the RGB crops are not excessively noisy. The estimators output each keypoint with an associated confidence score, upon which a visibility threshold can be applied to discard low-confidence predictions, thereby ensuring the quality of the extracted skeletons. Furthermore, when scenes are clear and involve a single subject, detectors can be leveraged to generate bounding boxes if not originally available. In multi-person scenarios, trackers may also be required to obtain the trajectories of each individual in the scene.

In summary, when RGB video sequences are available in an acceptable resolution and recorded under favorable conditions, only limited supervision is needed to derive skeleton annotations. A semi-automatic pose annotation pipeline is described in Appendix A, designed for use when the required resources are not directly provided, and thus build high-quality skeleton datasets from unannotated video data. In addition, we discuss further adjustments required to improve the integrity of the data.

Unfortunately, several challenges arise when directly working with these datasets:

  • Skeletal data issues: Although some datasets provide skeleton representations (e.g., J-HMDB, FallVision), these are offered in heterogeneous formats and often lack accuracy, as they have not been consistently subject to manual verification.
  • Camera angles and movements: Datasets such as Kinetics contain action clips captured from highly diverse, and frequently moving, viewpoints. This contrasts with CCTV scenarios, where cameras are generally fixed, with a restricted field of view and an overhead perspective.
  • Action dynamics: Many datasets, including all those listed in Table 2, feature simulated actions. When not carefully designed, these simulations tend to appear scripted, resulting in movements that lack naturalness and real-world variability.
  • Action ambiguity: Inconsistencies in the definition of actions are also common. For example, some datasets may not classify minor rotations without displacement as standing, or may exclude crouching from the sitting class, either separating it as an independent action or disregarding it entirely. Such inconsistencies can generate confusion and limit the diversity required for robust modeling.
  • Label granularity: Most datasets provide only clip-level labels, which often, whether intentionally or not, encompass multiple actions in addition to the one being annotated. For instance, some datasets label clips as falling while including numerous frames of preceding actions such as walking or sitting, as well as subsequent lying down.
  • Class imbalance: Many datasets present significant class imbalance. While combining datasets to mitigate this issue might be feasible, the heterogeneity in their characteristics and formats complicates the establishment of balanced class representations across multiple dimensions.

It is also worth mentioning more CCTV-oriented action datasets such as KISA [100], IKEA ASM [101], and HiEve [102], which are not considered in this work mainly due to limitations related to the specific actions of interest, licensing restrictions, or the practical impossibility of obtaining the data.

In practice, large-scale datasets such as Kinetics and NTU RGB+D serve as robust foundations for pretraining, and pretrained checkpoints for these are already accessible. For the specialization stage, however, attempting to combine any of the previous datasets while addressing their flaws and inconsistencies in order to obtain a balanced collection of samples that accurately captures the actions of interest, with sufficient intra-class variability and high-quality annotations, would be an exceedingly complex and resource-intensive undertaking. Although synthetic approaches [103] [104] have also recently been proposed to mitigate these limitations by generating balanced and diverse skeleton data, we instead opted to curate two dedicated datasets in our work: Le2iActions and CCTVActions , which are described in the following sections.

2.3.3 Le2iActions: An action-focused dataset

To begin with, our aim was to construct a dataset that captured precisely the set of actions defined in Section 2.1, while meeting as many of the quality requirements outlined above as possible. 2.3.3.1 Construction Process
For this purpose, we sought to use an existing dataset as a basis. Our task here is to obtain fine-grained sub-clips that capture the actions of interest, while preserving label granularity, avoiding ambiguity, and ensuring high-quality skeleton annotations. Nevertheless, some of the previously identified issues remain: motion patterns are not always realistic, as the basis dataset was created in a staged environment; certain contemplated cases may not be present in the segments to be extracted; and some sources of variability inherent to the final deployment scenario are also missing. Therefore, we named the new dataset Le2iActions, as it is effectively an extension of the original Le2i FDD dataset.

2.3.3.3 Window Size Selection

For training a classifier with sequences of skeletons, fixed-sized inputs will be required. In practice, we represent them as tensors of size $[W, K, C]$, where $C$ is the number of channels per keypoint (in principle $3$, encoding the 2D coordinates and a visibility mask), $K$ is the number of keypoints (already set to $17$), and $W$ is the number of frames, i.e., the window size. While some models can handle variable-length inputs at evaluation time, training is typically restricted to fixed dimensions. Since our system is designed for real-time processing, it is more consistent to adopt a fixed window size for both training and evaluation.

We argue that the most appropriate window size should be defined by the minimum number of frames required to reliably discern an action within our set of actions. While it could be determined experimentally, we instead base our choice on the characteristics of each dataset, assuming they approximate the temporal dynamics of the target actions in a general sense. Under this assumption, evaluating in real time with the selected window size is expected to be effective.

We select a single temporal window $W$ (in frames) by focusing on the transitional classes (falling, bending down, and getting up), since these events are time-critical and typically shorter, whereas continuous actions can be substantially truncated and still be recognized, as they provide sufficient discriminative evidence within many sub-segments.

Let $\{f_i\}_{i=1}^n$ be the frame counts of the $n$ transitional clips. For any candidate $W \in [\min_i f_i,\max_i f_i]$, define the coverage as

$$ C(W) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\lambda\,W \le f_i \le \mu\,W\,\right]. $$
Hence, $C(W)$ represents the fraction of transitional clips successfully captured by a window $W$, within a certain margin of padding or truncation. The specific strategy for padding and truncating sequences will be detailed in Section 2.4. The optimal window is then given by

$$ W^* = \arg\max_W C(W). $$

We set the tolerance multipliers to $\lambda=0.5$ and $\mu=2.0$, which define the admissible duration band: a clip is considered covered if its length lies between $50\%$ shorter and $100\%$ longer than $W$. This assumes that actions can still be recognized with as little as half their frames, or with up to an equal amount of additional padding. In this way, we avoid windows that are too short to capture the transitional (or continuous) events, while also preventing excessively long windows that would dilute the corresponding action signal.

Figure 12 illustrates the coverage curve based on the number of frames of transitional actions in the Le2iActions dataset. The optimal choice is $W^*=41$, which covers $97.6\%$ of clips and corresponds to approximately $1.64$ seconds at a frame rate of $25$ fps. For the sake of simplicity and clarity, we round this to $\mathbf{W=40}$ , which still covers $96.2\%$ of the clips.

If, for any reason (most likely efficiency), skeletons are not processed at the original frame rate, the input sequences are effectively subsampled. This must be taken into account when selecting the window size and applied consistently during training. For example, if every second frame is processed in production, this same subsampling should be reflected during training: instances may then have size $[W/2, K, C]$, with skeletons sampled every two frames. Optional temporal jitter may also be introduced, as noted in Section 2.4.

2.3.4.1 Construction Process

As there was no suitable dataset in the literature that matched our needs in terms of adaptability to CCTV scenarios, we created an entirely new dataset from scratch, leveraging the CCTV infrastructure owned by Innovasur. This infrastructure can be accessed in both real time and playback mode through the Avigilon Control Center Client software [105]. It comprises a network of CCTV cameras deployed across several areas in Spain, covering a variety of public spaces such as streets, parking lots, squares, roads, sidewalks, and indoor areas mainly within government buildings. The cameras operate at different heights, angles, formats, resolutions, and frame rates. Diversifying data collection across such heterogeneous sources enables the development of a system that can be deployed in a wide range of scenarios within this infrastructure or in similar ones.

Available CCTV footage

Given the time-consuming nature of the process, the involvement of the whole team was essential. Accordingly, a set of guidelines was established for data collection:

  • Each team member was responsible for gathering data from one or more specific areas (cities, towns, or industrial zones); in total, 8 areas were covered.
  • Within their assigned areas, members identified scenarios suitable for recording the target actions. For example, hallways or streets were appropriate for capturing walking, while waiting rooms or squares with benches were suitable for collecting sitting, bending down, or getting up sequences. No restrictions were imposed regarding camera characteristics or conditions, although preference was given to those providing relatively fluent video. In total, more than 100 distinct locations were selected.
  • Members then extracted clips from these scenarios, aiming to balance class representation, in line with the strict definitions provided in a detailed report which is summarized in Section 2.1.
  • They were instructed to capture actions from different subjects, under varying scales and occlusion levels, and at different times of day to reduce repetition. Extremely distant, poor-quality, or heavily occluded instances were discouraged.
  • Very crowded scenes were discouraged as well, since the presence of multiple people complicates post-processing for extracting skeleton data of a single individual. Classifier training is performed per identity, so additional subjects provide no extra benefit. Nonetheless, some crowded scenes were recorded for subsequent testing of the full pipeline.
  • Clip duration was required to span the entire action in the case of transitional actions, and to be at most 10 seconds for continuous actions, in order to facilitate semi-automatic pose annotation.

This process yielded a large number of clips across the target classes, capturing a wide range of scenarios, camera conditions, and subjects performing actions at a realistic pace (not staged), from different positions, trajectories, scales, and with varying levels of occlusion within the scene. Moreover, since instances were extracted on demand, we were able to achieve a perfectly balanced class distribution.

Staged Actions

However, another challenge had to be addressed. Actions such as falling or lying down are relatively uncommon, and we were unable to collect enough samples of these classes by reviewing existing CCTV footage. To overcome this limitation, we designed a plan to stage these actions. Specifically, additional clips were recorded in various accessible CCTV locations and then captured through the surveillance software. All actions were carried out safely and in a controlled environment, with protective measures such as the use of a mattress. The plan followed these guidelines:

  • We defined 20 subtypes of the falling action, including, for instance: frontal trip, falling face down (F-1), backward collapse due to dizziness (F-4), slipping on a wet floor (F-9), or falling after being hit (F-13). We also defined 8 subtypes of the lying down action, such as supine position (L-1) or side position, fetal-like (L-3). Additionally, we considered 4 extra subtypes of sitting that we found underrepresented after the main data collection, such as sitting on the floor (S-1) or kneeling (S-2). Finally, we also accounted for actions like getting up from the ground and bending down onto the ground.
  • Three volunteers performed the actions within a single day, across 10 different locations of a particular city.
  • Each volunteer wore different outfits, some chosen specifically to reduce visibility and create occlusion scenarios. Additional elements, such as people passing by or objects in the scene, were also introduced to enhance these effects.
  • Whenever possible, we adopted different positions and trajectories within the same scene to diversify the recordings.

We sought to balance all these aspects (subtype, subject, location, attire, and visibility), as well as some of their possible combinations, by logging each staged action in an Excel file that included graphs to illustrate real-time progress. This allowed the next simulation to be chosen strategically, ensuring that coverage goals were met. 2.4 Data Transformations and Augmentations

This section describes how skeleton sequences from the assembled datasets are presented to the classifier during both training and evaluation.

We define a set of deterministic transformations designed to standardize and normalize the inputs, ensuring consistency across samples at inference time while potentially restructuring the information in a way that could facilitate the formation of clearer decision boundaries during training.

In addition, the training stage incorporates a set of non-deterministic transformations, which essentially expand a single dataset instance into multiple samples, thereby performing online data augmentation. This set of data augmentations is designed to compensate for the scarcity of data, the intrinsic limitations of the datasets, and the expected conditions encountered in real-world scenarios, which may arise from the whole system's pipeline (Section 2.5). To ensure rigor and reproducibility in classifier evaluation, the stochastic components of these transformations are specifically controlled at this stage by fixing a random seed or specifying a particular configuration.

2.4.1 Spatial Normalization

A standard preprocessing step is to normalize 2D joint coordinates so as to reduce sensitivity to the original resolution and aspect ratio of the source frames from which skeleton annotations were derived. Given the frame height $H$ and width $W$, and raw keypoints $\{(x_i, y_i, s_i)\}_{i=1}^{K}$, where $(x_i, y_i)$ are the pixel coordinates of the $i$-th joint and $s_i \in \{0,1\}$ its associated visibility flag, we apply a fixed, frame-referenced mapping:

$$ \hat{x}_i = \frac{x_i - W/2}{W/2}, \qquad \hat{y}_i = \frac{y_i - H/2}{H/2}, \qquad \hat{s}_i = s_i, $$

which maps the domain $[0,W]\times[0,H]$ to $[-1,1]^2$, centering the frame at $(0,0)$. Keypoints with a visibility flag set to zero (i.e., discarded due to low confidence) are masked by setting their coordinates to $(0,0)$, thereby preventing unreliable joints from introducing noise into downstream models.

Normalization improves numerical conditioning and training stability. By design, it does not remove variability due to subject scale or position within the scene; it simply establishes a consistent coordinate space that makes samples comparable across frames and datasets.

2.4.2 Sequence Sampling

As discussed in Subsection 2.3.3.3 and Subsection 2.3.4.3, a fixed window size $W$ is chosen depending on the dataset(s) used, both for training and evaluation. Although evaluation would not strictly require this restriction, we enforce it from the beginning as it is the most robust design choice for subsequent real-time deployment. Furthermore, if the system is intended to operate at a lower frame rate than that of the original sequences, this window size should be adjusted accordingly (e.g., halved if every second frame is processed). Since the collected skeleton sequences have variable lengths, we must adopt a strategy to crop or pad them to the required size. The following aspects should be taken into account:
  • Frame interval: If the model is designed to handle skeleton sequences downsampled by a factor of $\lambda$, then $\lambda \times W$ skeletons must be considered from each of the existing sequences in order to subsample a final clip of length $W$. In other words, $\lambda \times W$ corresponds to the effective window size in terms of original frames.
  • Padding: If the effective window size exceeds the number of frames in a sequence, padding is required. Several strategies are commonly used, for example:
    • Zero-padding: Fill the sequence with empty skeletons (all keypoints set to zero) at the beginning or end.
    • Loop-padding: Repeat the entire sequence as many times as needed, truncating the excess if necessary.
    • Edge-padding: Extend the sequence by replicating the first or last skeleton until the desired length is reached.
    However, zero-padding introduces artifacts that do not reflect the inputs the model will encounter in a continuous stream. Loop-padding may mislead the model (e.g., a getting up action repeated in a loop could resemble the subject repeatedly getting up). Finally, edge-padding may bias the model toward patterns at the start or the end of the action.

    To address this, we designed a centered edge-padding strategy, which repeats the first and last skeletons as many times as needed to fill the sequence. For evaluation, padding is applied symmetrically, whereas during training it can be randomly shifted toward the beginning or the end. This yields a simple form of data augmentation, generating multiple variants of the shorter sequences and allowing the model to observe the same action in different temporal contexts. For example, a short falling action may be extended to include some standing frames at the beginning and lying down frames at the end, which provides a more realistic setting and teaches the model to recognize falls even when they occur within very short intervals.

  • Cropping: If the effective window size is smaller than the number of frames in a sequence, a crop of this length must be extracted. For evaluation, we always take the central crop, which should capture a complete action segment in continuous actions and at least the core part of the action in transitional ones, which are seldom much longer than the selected window size (as shown in the coverage analysis). For training, random crops are selected: in continuous actions they may correspond to different instances (e.g., a $300$-frame walking sequence may contain very different trajectories or paces across segments), while in transitional actions they expose the model to different temporal fragments, helping it learn to anticipate or delay predictions. In real-time operation, with continuous prediction over the last effective window, this should result in holding a transitional prediction for longer, which can aid action confirmation.
  • Temporal jitter: To further improve robustness to temporal variations, we may introduce a small random jitter when sampling frames from the original sequence. For instance, instead of taking every $\lambda$-th frame when downsampling, we select within a local window around it. This mimics momentary changes in action speed and frame drops due to stream latency, thus enhancing generalization.

2.4.3 Random Gaussian Noise

To improve robustness to small inaccuracies in joint localization, we inject random Gaussian noise into the normalized joint coordinates during training. For evaluation, we also add noise with a fixed random seed, specifically to make artificial padding frames less uniform and thus more natural, as they would otherwise remain identical across samples.

This Gaussian noise is added independently per frame and per keypoint, by sampling from a normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, where $\sigma$ is a parameter that controls the noise level. The perturbation is scaled to the spatial range of the skeleton within each frame and applied only to visible joints, leaving discarded ones unchanged. This design follows the frame-by-frame nature of pose estimation, where each skeleton is localized independently in time, thus simulating the realistic jitter that arises in keypoint detections.

2.4.4 Affine Transformations

Affine transformations such as scales, translations, and rotations can also be applied as forms of data augmentation. However, the CCTVActions dataset already captures a wide range of such variations, as shown in Figure 15. In addition, rotations require particular caution, as body orientation can be a discriminative and sometimes decisive factor when distinguishing between actions such as standing and lying down. For these reasons, we do not rely on these transformations in our pipeline.

2.4.5 Structured Joint Dropout

When building solutions based on skeletal data, we should consider that occlusions are common in real-world scenarios, especially in crowded scenes or when the subject interacts with objects. These occlusions can lead to missing keypoints, since it is difficult to determine the exact position of a joint that is not visible in the image. This is not only a possibility in practice, but also a limitation of the datasets we are using, as noted in Subsection 2.3.3.2.

We could argue that, for instance, a skeleton sequence missing both legs, when no displacement across the scene is observed, is more likely to correspond to a sitting action than to a standing one, as it is probably related to the presence of a table or similar furniture. However, such cases introduce bias into the model and may make the learning process easier, not because the network is truly learning the spatio-temporal nature of the actions, but because it is relying on shortcuts.

In addition, missing keypoints can also arise from inaccuracies in pose estimation, especially when lightweight models are used, and a classifier should exhibit robustness against these effects.

Numerous works in the literature [106] [107] [108] have proposed alternatives to address these issues. Some approaches even exploit the distribution of missing keypoints in the training set to guide the augmentation process [109]. In Algorithm 1, we define our own version of this strategy, which essentially drops joints out (i.e., sets their coordinates to zero) following a structured scheme.

This data augmentation relies on the following principles:

  • Seed sampling. The process begins by constructing a kill-set $Z$ of joints to be eliminated from the skeleton sequence. A seed joint $s$ is first drawn from a base probability map $p$ defined over all $K$ keypoints ($K=17$). Terminal joints (wrists and ankles) are given higher weights in this distribution than the rest; in particular, ankles are be more likely to be drawn than wrists. Probabilities are anatomically symmetric, so left and right counterparts share the same weight.
  • Region-based propagation. From the seed, joint selection propagates to other keypoints according to graph connectivity. The adjacency structure combines two criteria: (i) anatomical neighbors defined by the COCO skeleton edges (e.g., wrist–elbow–shoulder), and (ii) symmetric counterparts across the body (e.g., left–right ankles, knees, or wrists). This dual design is motivated by two factors: on the one hand, occlusions often affect spatially connected chains; on the other hand, pose estimators seem to encode a notion of symmetry and frequently fail simultaneously across symmetric parts, something we empirically noticed during data annotation.

    The propagation follows a breadth-first process in which, at each hop, the infection probability decays by a multiplicative factor $\delta \in (0,1]$. Smaller values restrict selection to local neighborhoods, whereas larger values allow entire limbs or mirrored regions to be eliminated.

    The bias of the first sampled joint towards terminal points, and especially the ankles, makes selection more likely to propagate along extremities, which are indeed the most commonly occluded in real-world scenarios. As a result, legs become the body parts most susceptible to elimination. This behavior is motivated by two observations: (i) the higher frequency of missing keypoints in the lower body due to occlusions and truncations in our datasets, and (ii) the strong discriminative role of the legs in actions such as sitting, bending down, or getting up. By assigning them higher elimination probabilities, the model is explicitly trained to be robust against occlusions in these critical regions.

    In contrast, a possible avenue for future work would be to assign higher elimination probabilities to head joints, not because such occlusions are common in deployment or in our datasets, but actually to reduce the model's reliance on these less informative joints (as discussed in Section 2.2), which might ultimately hinder classification.

  • Multiple zones. With probability $p_{\text{2nd}}$, a second independent seed is drawn and propagated, allowing more complex occlusion patterns, such as simultaneous missing arms and legs.
  • Skipping. With probability $p_{\text{skip}}$, the transformation is bypassed and the sample left unchanged. This ensures that a fraction of the data remains unaffected and prevents overexposure, since the augmentation is applied stochastically during training.
  • Temporal masking. Once $Z$ is fixed, elimination is applied frame by frame with probability $p_{\text{frame}}$. To enforce temporal coherence, if a frame is masked, the probability for the next frame is increased by a factor of $1.5$ (capped at $1$). This models the fact that real occlusions typically persist across consecutive frames rather than appearing independently.
  • Application. For the selected frames and joints, the corresponding coordinates and visibility masks are set to zero, simulating their complete absence in the skeleton sequence.

We define a suitable configuration for this data augmentation technique and empirically assess its impact on classifier performance in Subsection 3.3.4.

2.4.6 Feature Representation

Up to this point, we have defined all transformations as operating directly on a skeleton sequence, assumed to be a spatio-temporal graph with three channels per node: the $x$ and $y$ pixel coordinates of a keypoint, and its visibility mask. However, representing skeleton data through the joint features is not the only possible three-channel formulation.

We now introduce four alternative representations, each of which encodes information differently, allowing the model to exploit different aspects of the underlying dynamics. All of them are derived from the original joint representation, and although certain details of the raw input may be discarded, they are specifically designed to highlight more learnable patterns, while inevitably imposing constraints in some cases. Their effectiveness has been studied in several prior works [110] [111] [40] [112] [113].

Let $\mathbf{p}_{t,v}=(x_{t,v},y_{t,v})$ denote the 2D coordinates of joint $v$ at time $t$.

  • Bone (B). For this representation, we introduce the concept of a kinematic parent: each joint $v$ is associated with a parent joint $\pi(v)$, according to a hierarchical definition of the skeleton. This hierarchy reflects natural anatomical relations between body parts; for example, in the COCO configuration we find child–parent pairs such as knee–hip, wrist–elbow, or even hip–nose, which capture both symmetric limb relations and more global connections. Within this structure, the nose often plays a central role, acting as a reference point for balance and equilibrium.

    Based on this hierarchy, the bone vector is defined as the displacement from a joint to its parent:

    $$ \mathbf{b}_{t,v} = \mathbf{p}_{t,v} - \mathbf{p}_{t,\pi(v)}. $$
    Consequently, the feature channels are $(\mathbf{b}^x_{t,v},\, \mathbf{b}^y_{t,v},\, \mathbf{\bar{m}}_{t,v})$ for each joint $v$ and frame $t$, corresponding to the two vector components and the mean visibility of the joints that define the bone. This convention of averaging joint visibility will be consistently adopted in all subsequent representations. This representation encodes the second-order spatial structure of the body, implicitly capturing both the lengths and the orientations of its predefined kinematic segments. It introduces a natural invariance to any translations, including both absolute position within the scene and global motion of the subject over time, since features are defined as displacements relative to reference body parts within each frame. In other words, neither the location of the person in the image nor their overall movement across the scene can be recovered from this representation. As a consequence, it may lead to ambiguities between actions such as standing and walking, where relative leg or arm movements alone are not sufficient to reliably disambiguate the activity.

  • Joint Motion (JM). This representation encodes the temporal displacement of each joint between consecutive frames:
    $$ \Delta \mathbf{p}_{t,v} = \mathbf{p}_{t+1,v} - \mathbf{p}_{t,v}. $$
    The feature channels are $(\Delta \mathbf{p}^x_{t,v},\, \Delta \mathbf{p}^y_{t,v},\, \mathbf{\bar{m}}_{t,v})$, corresponding to the horizontal displacement, the vertical displacement, and the mean visibility of the joint across the two frames.

    In this way, JM captures the short-term velocity of individual joints, emphasizing how their positions evolve over time. This representation is naturally invariant to absolute positions within the frame, while still preserving information about whether the subject is moving or stationary throughout the sequence. As a result, actions such as walking and standing can be easily distinguished. On the other hand, information about relative joint configurations within a single frame is lost, making it often infeasible to discriminate between different static postures, such as standing, sitting, or lying down.

  • Bone Motion (BM). This representation measures the temporal displacement of bone vectors:
    $$ \Delta \mathbf{b}_{t,v} = \mathbf{b}_{t+1,v} - \mathbf{b}_{t,v}. $$
    The feature channels are then $(\Delta \mathbf{b}^x_{t,v},\, \Delta \mathbf{b}^y_{t,v},\, \mathbf{\bar{m}}_{t,v})$, corresponding to the horizontal displacement, the vertical displacement, and the mean visibility of the two joints that define the bone. BM captures dynamic variations in bone lengths and orientations, complementing JM by emphasizing structural rather than purely positional changes. Since it is derived from bones, this representation is also invariant to any kind of translations.
  • Geometric Bone (GB). When determining the label of an action from a sequence of skeletons without RGB context, factors such as size and absolute position of the subject are largely irrelevant. All previous representations already provide invariance to absolute position in the frame, but none of them achieve full invariance to scale, nor do they explicitly emphasize relative orientations of joints within a frame. Yet orientation is crucial for distinguishing between actions such as standing and lying down (a matter of global orientation, which is particularly discriminative under the consistent overhead, non-tilted view of most CCTV cameras), or sitting and bending down (a matter of local rotation involving leg joints).

    To address these issues jointly, we propose the GB representation, arising from the natural idea of expressing each bone vector in polar form. Similar ideas based on angular encodings have been explored in prior works [114] [115]. We compute it as

    $$ |\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t,v}| \;=\; \frac{\lVert \mathbf{b}_{t,v} \rVert_2} {\frac{1}{K}\sum_{v' \in K} \lVert \mathbf{b}_{t,v'} \rVert_2}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t,v} = \operatorname{atan2}(\mathbf{b}^y_{t,v},\, \mathbf{b}^x_{t,v}). $$

    The feature channels are thus $(|\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t,v}|,\, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t,v},\, \mathbf{\bar{m}}_{t,v})$, corresponding to the normalized bone length (with respect to the mean bone length across the subject in frame $t$), the orientation, and the mean visibility of the two joints. The per-frame, per-subject normalization ensures independence from scale changes over time while also making the approach suitable for real-time processing.

    In light of the above, we argue that this representation theoretically provides the most compact encoding for our particular set of actions, although empirical testing is still required. Nevertheless, it remains invariant to global motion over time, which makes it difficult to separate activities such as standing and walking; this, however, can be partially mitigated by considering the relative motion of legs and arms, although it is not a conclusive criterion.

As discussed, each representation entails its own advantages and limitations, and ideally they could be integrated to exploit their complementary strengths [40] [113]. In practice, however, we will empirically determine the single representation that best fits our solution (see Subsection 3.3.3).

After applying all of the transformations, skeleton sequences are represented as tensors $X \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times K \times C}$, where $W$ denotes the window size, $K=17$ the number of keypoints, and $C=3$ the feature channels. These tensors are then stacked into batches and fed to the classifier during both training and evaluation, enabling efficient parallel computation on GPUs.

2.5 Pipeline Definition

Once the data perspective has been established in terms of representation and sources, we can proceed to design the complete end-to-end action recognition system, specifically tailored to real-time CCTV surveillance scenarios. The pipeline comprises six main components, as anticipated: (i) a detector, (ii) a pose estimator, (iii) a tracker, (iv) a skeleton preprocessing module, (v) a classifier, and (vi) a prediction post-processing module. Throughout this section, we explain key architectural decisions by comparing different alternatives. We will also specify how data flows downstream, linking the components together. As a reference, works such as [116] and [117] describe closely related pose-based action recognition pipelines.

For a complete picture of the implementation of this pipeline and its precise configurations, refer to Chapter 4.

2.5.1 Detector

The first component of the pipeline is the person detector, which localizes every individual present in each frame coming from a real-time video stream by means of multi-object detection. Before inference, each frame is preprocessed, typically resized and normalized. Then, one-stage convolutional neural network–based detectors are employed, as they offer a favorable trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency. Representative architectures in this category include YOLO (You Only Look Once) [14] and SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector) [118], both of which achieve real-time operation at frame rates above 30 FPS on modern GPUs. Other alternatives include two-stage detectors such as Faster R-CNN [15], which often achieve higher accuracy by refining region proposals, or transformer-based models such as RT-DETR [119], which can better handle complex situations like occlusions. However, their higher computational cost generally makes them less suitable for real-time scenarios.

Essentially, one-stage detectors output bounding boxes around each detected person, usually defined by four parameters $(x, y, w, h)$ corresponding to the top-left corner coordinates and the width and height of the box, along with a confidence score indicating the likelihood that the enclosed region contains a person. These outputs are often presented as a tensor that also includes class probabilities for multi-class detection, with a size that depends on the specific architecture, as illustrated in Figure 20 for YOLO. In our design, however, the detector is constrained to the person class only, since additional object categories are irrelevant to human action recognition within the defined set of action categories.

Architecture of the original YOLO detector [14]. The input image is divided into an $S \times S$ grid, and each cell predicts $B$ bounding boxes, confidence scores, and $C$ class probabilities. Thus, the network outputs a tensor of size $S \times S \times (B \cdot 5 + C)$. In the above illustration, $S=7$, $B=2$, and $C=20$, yielding a final tensor of $7 \times 7 \times 30$.

Since detection constitutes the entry point of the pipeline, it can be regarded as its most critical component, as errors at this stage propagate downstream: a missed detection prevents the analysis of subject’s action sequence, while spurious detections introduce noise into pose estimation and classification. For this reason, our detector must be robust to variations in size, position, and at least moderate occlusions, for which pretrained models are particularly well suited. In addition, it must cope with unusual body configurations of our interest in surveillance scenarios, which may deviate from regular orientations, such as individuals in the midst of a falling or lying down event. It might be necessary to fine-tune detectors to perform reliably in such domain-specific conditions.

In contrast to dataset annotation (cf. Appendix A), where recall is initially prioritized, the focus here is on achieving a balanced precision–recall trade-off. To this end, some operational parameters must be carefully tuned to the deployment scenario:

  • Detection confidence: a minimum probability threshold ensures that only sufficiently reliable detections are propagated to the pose estimator.
  • Non-maximum suppression (NMS) threshold: NMS uses an IoU threshold $\tau_{\text{nms}}$ to suppress overlapping boxes. Lower $\tau_{\text{nms}}$ removes more overlaps (fewer duplicates but higher miss risk), while higher $\tau_{\text{nms}}$ retains more candidates (more redundancy). Note that if transformer-based detectors such as RT-DETR are employed, NMS is not required since duplicate removal is handled inherently within the architecture.
  • Maximum number of simultaneous people: this cap prevents the detector from flooding the system with detections in crowded scenes, where many individuals might be captured with varying levels of confidence.
  • Minimum detection size: very small bounding boxes are typically associated with distant or spurious detections; filtering them reduces noise and computational overhead.
  • Privatization layers (region masking): in fixed-camera environments, regions of the frame that are irrelevant or problematic for human action recognition can be excluded from processing at this step. Examples include crowded background areas, static posters containing human figures, or points of perspective convergence where false detections frequently arise.

From an efficiency standpoint, the system can be optimized by adjusting the frame interval, i.e., running the detector every $N$ frames instead of on each individual frame. Proper coordination with downstream components is essential to preserve both accuracy and real-time responsiveness when temporal context is deliberately subsampled.

2.5.2 Pose Estimator

Once a person has been localized in a particular frame, their pose is estimated using a top-down approach. This involves cropping the image to each Region of Interest (ROI) provided by the detector and applying a specialized algorithm that predicts the coordinates of the human skeleton keypoints (as defined in Section 2.2), along with a confidence score for each point. To generate the crop, the detection tensor must first be processed, optionally adding some padding to ensure a uniform input size for the pose estimator, which also provides additional context that may be valuable (e.g., the extended bounding box may include an arm partially outside the original bounding box). Regarding the output, it may be produced as heatmaps for each joint, matching the input resolution. These heatmaps can then be straightforwardly post-processed to extract the $(x,y)$ coordinates of each keypoint.

In contrast, bottom-up methods (e.g., OpenPose [16]) detect all keypoints across the entire image and then group them by individual. Although this removes the need for an explicit detection stage, it typically demands a far more precise initial understanding of the scene, which is difficult to guarantee in complex CCTV environments. In addition, adapting such methods to real-time pipelines is considerably more challenging. For these reasons, top-down approaches are generally preferred, as their stepwise design processes each individual separately, reducing ambiguities among nearby people and ultimately achieving higher accuracy.

As a top-down pose estimator, we can employ convolutional neural networks trained on large-scale human pose datasets such as COCO [72] or MPII [74]. These models typically rely on a convolutional backbone to extract features from the cropped ROI, followed by a prediction head that generates the joint heatmaps. Prominent examples include HRNet (High-Resolution Network) [80] and Simple Baseline [120], as well as the transformer-based ViT-Pose [78], built upon the ViT architecture [121]. The latter has proven highly effective during data annotation; although computationally more demanding than CNN-based alternatives, lighter variants have been developed that can be adapted for real-time operation while maintaining a favorable precision–throughput trade-off (see Figure 21). Nevertheless, it is important to note that employing such models, while providing a substantial performance gain, also makes the pose estimation module the heaviest component of the pipeline.

Visualization of ViTPose and other state-of-the-art methods on the COCO validation set, highlighting the trade-off between precision, throughput, and model complexity. Bubble size encodes the number of parameters. Image obtained from [78].

Note that it is also possible to fuse detection and pose estimation into a single stage, as done in models such as YOLO-Pose [79] or RTMO [122]. While these approaches are practical and computationally efficient, we argue that they generally yield less accurate pose estimations than top-down models operating directly on person crops, since the joint task of detection and pose regression is inherently more complex. Moreover, maintaining two separate modules provides greater flexibility and simplicity in optimizing or improving each component independently. For example, if the pipeline fails to detect persons unusual postures, it is easier to fine-tune or replace only the detector rather than retrain the entire one-stage architecture, which would additionally require keypoint annotations for such atypical postures.

After processing the heatmaps and extracting joint coordinates, it is necessary to re-project them onto the original image reference frame, since the estimator outputs positions relative to the cropped ROI. Confidence scores are then thresholded to discard unreliable keypoints, producing visibility masks. This procedure yields a single normalized skeleton associated with each detected individual.

2.5.3 Tracker

It is essential to preserve the identity of each individual over time, enabling their bounding boxes and corresponding poses to be associated across consecutive frames and assembled into consistent trajectories (tracks). The tracking module receives as input the detections from each frame, namely the coordinates and bounding box positions, and establishes correspondences across successive frames to maintain identity continuity over time. This task, commonly referred to as multi-object tracking, is typically addressed in two principal ways, or through a combination of both: by exploiting spatial and geometric proximity between detections in consecutive frames (e.g., IoU-based or motion-based trackers) or by leveraging re-identification (ReID) through appearance features.

  • Motion-based trackers. Lightweight approaches such as SORT [123] rely on bounding box overlap (IoU) and Kalman filtering [124] to associate detections across frames under the assumption of limited displacement. They are computationally efficient but prone to identity switches when trajectories intersect or when detections are missing. More recent methods, such as ByteTrack [125], retain this efficiency while achieving state-of-the-art performance.
  • Appearance-based trackers. These methods leverage visual features to preserve identity under pose or motion changes. Examples include DeepSORT [126], BoT-SORT [127], and correlation-filter families such as DCF/CSR-DCF [128]. They associate detections through embeddings or filter responses, often combined with motion cues. By exploiting appearance (e.g., clothing or color), these trackers are more robust to occlusion and trajectory overlap, albeit at a higher computational cost.
  • Pose-based trackers: Trackers that exploit skeleton information to improve identity association can also be considered. Rather than relying exclusively on bounding boxes, these methods measure pose similarity, for instance by computing the distance between joint coordinates across consecutive frames. Representative examples include the LightTrack framework [129] and the KeyTrack method [81]. Although conceptually appealing, we discard this option since pose-based trackers are still less mature and would be difficult to integrate into our current system.

The tracker can also be placed before the pose estimator and synchronized with the detector, so that detections are not performed on every frame. Instead, the module would interpolate bounding boxes (or poses, when applicable) by leveraging identity information across subsampled frames, thereby enabling skeleton estimation to be carried out likewise on each frame while still improving overall efficiency. This strategy is natively supported by the implementation framework, as described in Chapter 4. However, when detections are skipped for efficiency, pose inference may also be omitted, as it typically constitutes the heavier component. In this case, interpolation becomes unnecessary, since bounding boxes are used primarily to generate crops for pose estimation (and, optionally, for real-time visualization).

2.5.4 Skeleton Preprocessing

This module aggregates the information and applies the necessary transformations required for classifier processing at a specified frequency. Specifically, at a given instant, it stacks the last $W$ skeletons associated with each tracked individual and stores them in a tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times 17 \times 3}$, which encodes the temporal sequence of skeletal features, such as 2D joint coordinates and their corresponding visibility masks. $W$ corresponds to the selected window size for the classifier, in line with Subsection 2.3.3.3 and Subsection 2.3.4.3. A conceptual overview of how such sequences are constructed and fed to the subsequent classifier is illustrated in Figure 22.

The pose visualizations are provided, but are not used as RGB inputs; they simply illustrate the underlying structured tensors.

For the sequence to encode high-quality information that can be further processed, it is crucial that the three previous modules work and interact seamlessly. Additionally, we apply some of the transformations defined in Section 2.4, further enhancing the input data:

  • Absolute skeleton coordinates are normalized to the $[-1,1]$ range.
  • Each keypoint is converted into the selected representation format from Subsection 2.4.6.
  • No explicit padding or truncation is required, as we always collect the last $W$ skeletons. If a subject has not yet accumulated enough skeletons, it will not be eligible for action classification. During evaluation, deterministic noise was added precisely to assess the performance of this operating mode while still allowing for sequences shorter than $W$ in the validation and test sets.

Other preprocessing strategies include the Optical Flow Pose Image (OFPI) [130], which incorporates optical flow as pseudo-images representing temporal joint trajectories to better capture motion dynamics, and the Encoded Human Pose Image (EHPI) [131], which encodes joint coordinates into an image-like format using color channels for different joints. However, although these approaches are interesting and enable further processing through CNNs, they forfeit the compactness and structure of skeleton representations and require generating RGB images in real time, which is neither trivial nor computationally affordable when taking into account the rest of the components.

It is important to note that the last $W$ poses for an individual may not correspond to the last $W$ frames, hence frame–pose consistency is not fully guaranteed. Tracks may be interrupted if a previously detected individual is temporarily lost or if the stream becomes unstable. This situation is inherently unpredictable, but can be exposed to the model through temporal augmentations. However, if frame loss is systematic (for instance, when pose inferences are intentionally skipped for efficiency), we should ensure that the subsequent classifier is trained on subsampled windows of smaller size, or that the original frame rate is sufficiently high so that the effective processing rate is still captured within the chosen window size (see Subsection 2.3.4.3). In general, when using the same classifier across cameras with different frame rates while skipping frames in all of them, it is advisable to train the model to encode this skipping strategy consistently across samples, thereby ensuring robustness.

Another possible option would be to interpolate missing skeletons, which, if done correctly, could establish a correspondence between wall-clock time and the number of poses in a sequence. A classifier could then be trained under the same strategy, so the system encodes a single notion of time, effectively eliminating one source of variability. Temporal differences would thus only arise from action speeds, which simplifies the recognition task. However, we choose to maintain the previous strategy to avoid the potential uncontrollability of skeletons, as skeleton interpolation can be noisier than bounding box interpolation and requires on-the-fly skeleton generation, which incurs an efficiency cost.

2.5.5 Classifier

The action classification module takes each skeleton sequence as input, produces class probability maps over the selected actions, and batches the predictions per identity. The earlier steps merely serve to prepare the data, whereas this stage performs the actual action recognition, and therefore requires more tuning and careful training.

As stated in Section 2.2, data are represented as graph-based pose sequences, including both anatomical and temporal connections, each associated with an action label. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) are neural architectures designed to process graphs as input, learning compact representations from their structured connectivity, as described in Section 1.3. These models encode the relationships between joints either explicitly, using predefined anatomical and temporal adjacencies provided with the tensor built in the skeleton preprocessing module, or implicitly, by learning abstractions of joint correlations from the training set. The resulting representations are then passed to a multilayer perceptron to produce normalized class probabilities through a softmax activation function, as shown in Figure 22.

Many GCN variants have been proposed in the literature for skeleton-based action recognition, such as ST-GCN [24], CTR-GCN [132], and ProtoGCN [41], the latter introducing attention-based mechanisms. The most suitable model for our pipeline will be selected in Subsection 3.3.2. These models are typically lightweight, since they operate on relatively small inputs compared to RGB images, which allows real-time execution. Nevertheless, predictions are inherently delayed, as they require the analysis of the most recent $W$ skeletons. Our padding and truncation strategy during training (see Subsection 2.4.2), however, may help mitigate this delay by enabling earlier predictions.

Different paradigms for encoding spatio-temporal pose graphs include recurrent models such as RNNs (e.g., LSTMs and GRUs) [133] [134] [135], which model temporal dynamics via hidden state vectors. However, these approaches generally lack an explicit understanding of the skeleton's spatial structure, and joints are often concatenated as plain vectors. Yet these methods primarily focus on temporal continuity rather than preserving the anatomical graph topology. Moreover, RNN-based models are typically tailored to variable-length sequences, whereas our approach is adapted to rely on a predefined window of fixed length to capture each action.

Along with skeletal graphs, a second stream of RGB sequences can be constructed and fed to the classifier, as shown in [136] [137]. In some cases, optical flow is also considered, for example in [138] (though in that work skeletons are processed as images via 3D-CNNs). While such additional streams can increase discriminative power, we opt not to use them, as the actions described in Section 2.1 do not require such dense visual cues. Furthermore, they may introduce noise that would demand more training data and add complexity to an already challenging pipeline. A similar trade-off arises when attempting to ensemble different GCN architectures to obtain averaged predictions [40] [113]. Although this strategy could leverage complementary views of the same data, it also increases the computational and implementation burden.

2.5.6 Prediction Post-processing

The final component of the pipeline is responsible for refining the raw predictions of the classifier for each identity. To produce the final outputs of the action recognition system as robustly as possible, we can apply several techniques:

  • Sliding window: The classifier generates predictions over windows of size $W$, typically matching the length of the sequences produced in the skeleton preprocessing module, advancing with a stride $s$ that is defined relative to the pose inference frequency (i.e., the number of skeletons between two consecutive prediction windows). For each skeleton-inference instant $t$, the final class score is obtained by aggregating the predictions of all windows overlapping at that instant. For this purpose, a small queue of window predictions is maintained. To avoid unreliable contributions from border cases, we introduce a margin $\mu$ (in skeletons, aligned with the frame rate), so that windows where $t$ lies too close to the edges are excluded.

    This scheme induces a theoretical bounded delay, since the prediction for $t$ is only fully available after the last relevant window is processed. The maximum latency can be expressed as $L_{max} = W - \mu + s - 1$. Although it may introduce some additional time overhead, the method smooths the prediction signal and ensures that each decision is supported by sufficient temporal context. This strategy has been proposed in works such as [139], whose method is illustrated in Figure 23, and is overall very similar to our approach. The only substantial difference is that we average the prediction scores instead of applying majority voting, thereby preserving the probability distribution rather than discarding it in favor of the highest class, which may be ambiguous.

    Sliding-window voting scheme from [139]. Each window of length $W$ (stride $SW_{\text{step}}$) yields a class prediction $A_k$. At each time $t$, overlapping windows vote in $\hat{o}_t$, and the final label $\hat{f}_t$ is given by majority vote.

    However, we still face issues such as flickering (rapid label changes), fragmentation (split segments for the same action), and merging (different actions fused together) due to overlapping predictions. These artifacts are difficult to eliminate: post-processing with explicit smoothing requires future information (incurring additional delay), or it demands much more complex training schemas, like those defined in [140] or [141].

    Following this post-processing technique, we can populate a confidence memory vector, i.e., a stabilized class-probability representation over a fixed long window, which may retain either the full distribution or only the most likely class at each prediction step. This memory vector can then be inspected periodically to log people counts and their corresponding performed actions, enabling the generation of statistical reports.

  • Confidence filtering: Once the aggregated prediction at a given instant is obtained, we can apply confidence thresholds to determine whether a predicted action should be considered tentative, confirmed, or ignored.
  • Logical constraints: Simple monotonic restrictions can be imposed on the sequence of actions. For example, if we know that a certain action (e.g., lying down) can only occur after others (e.g., bending down, falling), post-processing can be used to enforce such temporal consistency. To this end, the confidence memory vector is analyzed: if a given action remains within the confirmed range for a sufficient portion of recent frames (e.g., 90% of the last 30 frames), the sliding window output for the next instant can be restricted to the subset of actions that can logically follow it. This approach is feasible under the assumption of consistently high top-2/top-3 accuracy, which is indeed observed in our experiments.
  • Generation of events: Similar to the previous point, the confidence memory vector can be monitored to detect chains of actions that may trigger an alarm. For instance, a partial persistence of one confirmed action (e.g., falling) followed by another (e.g., lying down) can provide sufficient evidence to raise an alert. Naturally, the number of frames required should be calibrated according to the camera's frame rate and any deliberately skipped frames (i.e., the effective frame rate), while the remaining parameters are best determined empirically during deployment.

Many additional techniques could be applied to support final predictions and event generation, but the trade-off between complexity, latency, and effectiveness must be carefully considered. For example, lying down predictions could be assisted by criteria involving the aspect ratio of bounding boxes under a fixed overhead camera. However, introducing such biases and handcrafted rules often bring additional intricacy and may degrade overall performance. Indeed, logical constraints are conceptually sound but difficult to implement efficiently, and are therefore left for future work, while the remaining techniques have been integrated into the final system described in Chapter 4.

3.2 Default Setup

Neural network training involves a multitude of hyperparameters and design choices. In order to maintain consistency across experiments and isolate the variables identified as critical to our analysis, we define a default setup that serves as baseline. Unless stated otherwise, the following configurations will be applied:

  • Optimizer: Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with Nesterov momentum of 0.9 and weight decay of $5 \times 10^{-4}$, following standard practices in the literature [13] [149] [150]. Gradient clipping is not applied.
  • Early stopping: For experiments involving a single validation set, early stopping is triggered when no improvement is observed in the validation macro-F1 score over 5 consecutive epochs.

    For experiments that use cross-validation, early stopping is deliberately omitted in both of its possible variants: stopping independently within each fold, which would introduce bias in the learning curves by terminating training at different epochs; and stopping based on a global validation criterion, which would break partition independence and introduce information leakage between folds.

  • Data preparation: Skeleton keypoint coordinates are first normalized to the range $[-1, 1]$. Crops of 30 consecutive frames are then extracted from each sequence, extended to 40 when working exclusively with Le2iActions. These crops are sampled randomly during training, and deterministically during validation and testing. To enhance variability, Gaussian noise ($\sigma=0.001$) is injected into the keypoints during training. All these steps are described in detail in Section 2.4.
  • Pretraining: The models are initialized with weights trained and evaluated on the NTU RGB+D 120 [91] Cross-Subject split (XSub), using HRNet 2D poses extracted directly from the RGB stream (rather than the original Kinect 3D joints), providing a strong foundation for downstream development.   • Metrics: At each epoch, we compute the following measures on both the training and validation sets:
    • Loss : objective function value that the model seeks to minimize, dependent on the specific model (e.g., cross-entropy).
    • Top-1 accuracy : percentage of samples for which the predicted label matches the ground truth.
    • Top-2 accuracy : percentage where the correct label is among the two most confident predictions. This metric is especially informative in a 7-class setting, where a good classifier is expected to place the correct label at least in the top two, even if some confusion arises (e.g., between standing and lying down).
    For the validation set, we also track:
    • Macro-F1 score : balances precision ($\tfrac{TP}{TP+FP}$) and recall ($\tfrac{TP}{TP+FN}$), averaging them equally across classes:

      This prevents majority classes from dominating the evaluation.
    • Confusion matrix : records per-class prediction counts, highlighting systematic misclassifications and showing which classes are most often confused.
    All the above metrics are also available for the final test set.

Some aspects of the setup are inherited directly from the recommendations and default templates provided by the PYSKL repository, ensuring alignment with best practices in the domain.

3.3 Experimental Design Factors

We examine several factors that may affect performance in our context:

  • Training dynamics, comprising both the training strategy and the associated hyperparameters. The strategy defines how the two datasets are combined to optimize learning, either in a single stage or in two stages (e.g., finetuning (FT), linear probing (LP), or their combinations), always starting from the aforementioned knowledge base. Since optimal hyperparameters such as learning rate or batch size may vary across strategies, as noted in [151], we tune them individually for each approach, before selecting the most promising strategy overall.
  • Architecture, i.e., the choice of backbone model. We compare a set of lightweight graph-based architectures, seeking a balance between accuracy and real-time performance.
  • Input modality, i.e., the type of skeleton-based features provided to the model. Options include joint coordinates, bone vectors, and motion-based representations, which have been shown to capture complementary aspects of human actions.
  • Data augmentations and transformations applied to the input skeletons. These aim to enhance robustness by increasing variability and mitigating dataset limitations.

We consider these four aspects to be sufficiently independent to be studied in isolation. This assumption is supported by prior works that treat model design decisions as separable dimensions for systematic analysis, such as [152] or [153]. While interplay between factors inevitably exists, certain patterns support this particular separation: for example, an adequate set of data augmentations or an improved input representation would generally benefit different architectures in a similar way, and convergence behaviour often depends more on dataset properties than on the specific backbone, unless architectural differences are large enough to alter learning dynamics.

Each aspect is addressed in a dedicated stage, following a design akin to an ablation study, where only the components relevant to that stage are varied while the rest remain fixed. This provides a fair and practical framework for a clean, progressive study, where the configuration selected in each stage is carried forward to the next, and known intra-factor dependencies (e.g., correlations between learning rate and batch size) are explicitly considered. This methodology also avoids a combinatorial explosion in computational requirements which, in practice, may not even lead to the most effective analytical strategy [154].

3.3.1 Training Dynamics

Guided by this staged design, we first focus on training dynamics, since they govern the optimization process and determine the conditions under which later choices will be evaluated.

3.3.1.1 Definition of Training Strategies
1-FT
Finetune with a merged dataset. We mix Le2iActions and CCTVActions, so the model jointly learns from both sources. However, validation is performed using exclusively the latter.
2-FT
Finetune with Le2iActions, then finetune with CCTVActions. The rationale for this approach is that the first dataset provides a solid idea of the actions to learn, while the second one allows for further specialization in the context of CCTV. This strategy is often discussed within the scope of progressive or multi-stage transfer learning [155] [156].
LP-FT
Linear probe with Le2iActions, then finetune with CCTVActions. We consider this strategy as relevant, as the literature cites it as the most effective [151] [157] [158].
FT-LP
Finetune with Le2iActions, then linear probe with CCTVActions. Although it may seem intuitive to first learn a more action-specific representation through finetuning, and then adapt to the deployment-specific dataset by adjusting only a few parameters (specifically the final linear layer), this is not a common practice in transfer learning setups. Nevertheless, we include this strategy in our study and subsequently reflect on it.

We discard the option of training from scratch, as overfitting would arise due to the relatively small size of our datasets. We also exclude strategies based solely on linear probing, whether in one or two stages, as preliminary experiments showed that features extracted from the pretrained backbone do not adapt sufficiently to our task, requiring a greater degree of adjustment, as expected. Other strategies, such as integrating LP and FT in a single stage [159] or selectively freezing a subset of layers [160] or are also overlooked for simplicity.

3.3.1.2 General Hyperparameter Settings

Standard hyperparameters used for tuning neural networks, such as learning rate or batch size, are crucial for achieving optimal performance. Their selection is typically performed through trial and error, or by adopting default values from the literature. However, the appropriate choice may depend on the specific task, and in our case the most suitable values are unclear due to the particularities of the problem. Given that we have constructed datasets of limited size and our architectures are lightweight, computation times are relatively short. Therefore, we can afford to perform a grid search over some of these hyperparameters to find a reasonable configuration for our models at different training stages.

The relevant hyperparameters considered in this study, along with their corresponding ranges, are:

  • Base learning rate (LR): $\{10^{-2},\ 10^{-3},\ 10^{-4}\}$.

    This hyperparameter controls the optimiser's step size at each update. The selected values span two orders of magnitude, allowing exploration of both fast and conservative training regimes: higher rates may speed up convergence but risk instability, while lower ones favour stable but slower updates.

  • Learning rate scheduler policy: { Fixed with warmup, One Cycle, Reduce On Plateau }.

    These strategies adjust the learning rate dynamically during every training iteration. See Figure 25 for a visual comparison.

    • Fixed with warmup: brief linear warmup (3 epochs starting at 1% of the target LR) to stabilize early updates [161], increasing to the base LR and keeping it constant thereafter.
    • One Cycle: LR rises to the base value at epoch 3, then cosine-anneals to a much smaller value until epoch 20 (beyond that point, it remains fixed); initial and final LR are \(1/100\) and \(1/1000\) of the peak, respectively. This policy is closely linked to super-convergence [162], enabling fast and robust training within fewer epochs.
    • Reduce On Plateau: LR remains fixed at the base value unless validation macro-F1 stalls, then it is halved after 2 stagnant epochs, allowing longer plateaus when useful and adaptively lowering LR as learning saturates.
    We fixed absolute epoch values (e.g., for reaching the LR peak or minimum) based on preliminary experimental observations, which, for instance, indicated that 20 epochs are generally sufficient for convergence. Accordingly, we set 50 epochs as a hard upper bound for the total training budget.

    Comparison of learning-rate schedules. The curves were obtained from real training runs, with per-epoch logging, which explains the minor irregularities.

  • Batch size: $\{16,\ 32,\ 64\}$.

    We study how these widely used values influence convergence behaviour, with particular focus on their effect over gradient estimation and training stability.

  • Dataset repetitions: $\{1,3,5\}$.

    Repeating the dataset $r$ times multiplies the number of optimisation steps per epoch by $r$ while keeping the batch size fixed, yielding $r$ stochastically augmented views of each sample per epoch. This is related to batch augmentation [163]: whereas batch augmentation duplicates views within a mini-batch, repetitions distribute them across iterations, sometimes placing multiple views in the same batch due to shuffling, lengthening the epoch and (with epoch-based schedulers) altering the effect of the LR schedule. We include this factor to quantify how extra exposure and scheduler interactions affect convergence and generalisation in low-data regimes.

  • Dropout: $\{0,\ 0.3,\ 0.5\}$.

    The range spans from no regularisation to moderate standard values [164]. For non-zero values, dropout deactivates neurons during training, which can promote redundancy in representations and reduce co-adaptation, thus potentially improving model robustness.

Although incorporating cross-validation to improve the robustness of our results would be feasible, we simplify the procedure since this study is merely exploratory, fixing a randomly chosen fold as the validation set. Ultimately, our goal is to determine an effective set of hyperparameters for training, but we do not require fine-grained results to identify an adequate configuration. We recognize that noise and randomness inevitably affect outcomes, so we focus on general trends in the behaviour of individual factors or their combinations, rather than on exact values, and let our observations guide the analysis. Throughout this process, some of the factors may be deemed settled with appropriate justification, allowing their values to be fixed and omitted from further analysis.

As stated earlier, we will carry out a separate study for each of the training strategies defined in Subsection 3.3.1, considering the different stages within each case, as they might present distinct convergence patterns. To ensure a fair comparison, the rest of the experimental setup is fixed, assuming these factors have limited impact on training dynamics, particularly on the pace and stability of convergence across epochs. Alongside the default setup described in Section 3.2, we adopt ST-GCN++ [145] as the graph convolutional backbone, using the cross-entropy loss and feeding it directly with joint-level inputs (2D coordinates together with visibility indicators for each keypoint). 3.3.2 Architecture

Now, we turn to the choice of graph convolutional architecture while keeping the setup established so far fixed. We evaluate four representative models, characterized as follows:

  • ST-GCN++ [145]: improved ST-GCN [24] with multi-scale temporal modules and residual connections, widely adopted as a strong baseline.
  • CTR-GCN [132]: introduces channel-wise topology refinement, dynamically learning correlations per channel and sample, achieving state-of-the-art results on large benchmarks.
  • AAGCN [113]: employs attention-based adaptive adjacency matrices to capture action-dependent correlations beyond fixed skeleton topology.
  • ProtoGCN [41]: a very recent approach that leverages prototype-based representations to align skeleton embeddings with action prototypes, enhancing generalization in low-data or fine-grained scenarios, and clearly achieving the best results on NTU RGB+D (60/120) and Kinetics-Skeleton (see Table 1) to date.
Some of these models introduce implicit connectivity mechanisms, which are less relevant in our case since the skeleton layout is fixed, but can still yield performance gains by capturing richer dependencies between joints. 3.3.4 Data Augmentation

Finally, we assess the impact of data augmentation. The training baseline already incorporates simple augmentations such as padding, truncation, and Gaussian noise. Scaling and translation variability are effectively eliminated by the geometric bone representation. Orientation, by contrast, is explicitly exploited by this representation, and artificially altering it may be detrimental, since global orientations are already implicitly encoded in the CCTVActions dataset, as the cameras record from an overhead, fixed-angle, CCTV-like perspective, which is inherent to our target application.

Therefore, we focus on evaluating the effect of the Structured Joint Dropout transformation (see Subsection 2.4.5) under different settings, which encourages resilience to occlusions and pose estimation failures, and is expected to induce the network to genuinely learn the underlying motion patterns across different contexts. Four settings are considered, varying the parameters of infection decay $\delta$ (which controls the likelihood of larger zones being eliminated), skipping probability $p_{\text{skip}}$ (which determines the proportion of augmented samples per epoch), and temporal masking $p_{\text{frame}}$ (which specifies the probability of masking individual frames within each sequence):

  • Low: $\delta=0.25$; $p_{\text{skip}}=0.7$; $p_{\text{frame}}=0.4$
  • Medium: $\delta=0.5$; $p_{\text{skip}}=0.6$; $p_{\text{frame}}=0.6$
  • High: $\delta=0.75$; $p_{\text{skip}}=0.5$; $p_{\text{frame}}=0.8$
  • Very High: $\delta=1$; $p_{\text{skip}}=0.4$; $p_{\text{frame}}=1$
The initial masking probabilities are fixed such that ankles are $75\%$ more likely to initiate the infection process than wrists, and at least an order of magnitude more likely than any other body part, in alignment with real occlusion patterns and dataset characteristics. The probability of infecting a secondary zone is set to $p_{\text{2nd}}=0.05$, except in the Very High configuration, where it is increased to $0.2$. We also compare these settings against a No Dropout baseline.

In this case, we present in Figure 36 the cross-validation top-1 accuracy learning curves, for the training (left) and validation (right) cross-validated sets.

On the validation side, the model appears to require slightly more epochs to converge as the intensity of joint elimination increases, although the values attained towards the final epochs are largely comparable across configurations. In the training curves, it is evident that higher levels of joint removal lead the metrics to reach a plateau earlier. This is a desirable effect: increased data variability makes the training process more challenging, rendering the model less prone to overfitting. This effect is particularly pronounced in the Very High setting. However, under this most aggressive configuration the model fails to reach the same performance on the validation set, due to the severity of the dropout, which is designed to eliminate larger zones and affects roughly $60\%$ of the samples. In addition, we argue that this setting may be suboptimal, as eliminating keypoints across all skeletons in each sample of each sample through $p_{\text{frame}}=1$ does not realistically capture the per-frame uncertainty of pose estimation, which in turn can lead to unnatural “blinking keypoints” in real scenarios.

Thus, we choose to retain the High Structured Joint Dropout configuration, as it provides a good balance while effectively reducing overfitting (even achieving the highest validation top-1 accuracy). It is also plausible that, given additional epochs in the final model training stage, this setting could achieve further performance gains. The relatively high number of dataset repetitions (5) further reinforces the effect of data augmentation, as it exposes the model to diverse augmented samples in every epoch, which in turn positively influences convergence.

3.4 Training a Final Model

After selecting the most suitable configuration for our problem through cross-validation, and justifying each design choice with both empirical and theoretical arguments, we proceed to the final training stage to obtain the best possible model instance.

For this purpose, we fix one of the partitions from CCTVActions previously used in cross-validation and designate it as the validation set for the final training. This results in approximately $67\%$ of the CCTVActions dataset being used for training, given the 85:15 train/test split combined with 5-fold partitioning; in the case of Le2iActions, $85\%$ of the dataset is used, since its entire training split is employed at this stage.

Other alternatives could have been considered, such as holding out a dedicated validation set from CCTVActions from the beginning (in addition to the test set), or using its entire 85% training split without any validation set. However, the safest strategy to avoid overfitting is to retain a validation set from the CCTVActions folds and apply early stopping based on validation macro-F1, selecting the model checkpoint that performs best on validation (provided its performance remains consistent with training metrics), even though this approach sacrifices the opportunity to fully exploit the data for learning.

3.6 Explainability Analysis

In classification tasks with graph neural networks (GCNs), such as our case of human action recognition from skeleton sequences, it is crucial to understand which parts of the graph (nodes, edges, or attributes) influence the network's predictions. Models like CTR-GCN learn spatial and temporal patterns in the skeleton graph to classify actions, but they initially operate as black boxes. Although one could argue that our final model is already somewhat interpretable, verifying this claim requires the application of dedicated explainability techniques. These methods make it possible to confirm that the model focuses on logically relevant aspects of the movement, thereby increasing trust in the system, and to extract insights into the motion dynamics that characterize each action class.

In this case, we apply two post-hoc, path-based feature attribution methods described in Section 1.5: Integrated Gradients (IG) [50] and its extension, Expected Gradients (EG) [57], implemented via [58]. We select these techniques because (a) they satisfy important integrity axioms that most other methods do not, (b) they are applicable to any differentiable function, including GCNs, and (c) they are computationally efficient and straightforward to implement, as they do not require additional training or architectural modifications to the already trained and evaluated model. Both methods operate on a spatio-temporal skeleton graph, assigning an importance score to each node (which in our case corresponds to a geometric bone) with respect to the model output (here taken as the post-softmax class probabilities).

For this study, we select a few representative samples from the CCTVActions test set across different classes and analyze their attribution maps in spatial, temporal, and spatio-temporal dimensions. For brevity, only a limited number of visualizations are included. We also discuss the baseline selection problem, which is critical for IG, as well as the stability of EG and a brief evaluation of sensitivity to feature elimination.

3.6.1 Baseline Selection

To use IG effectively, it is advisable to select a suitable general baseline. A common choice in the literature is a signal-free input (i.e., an input that generates close-to-zero outputs). Several alternatives can be considered:

  • Zero baseline: A skeletal sequence with all input channels set to zero. Still, this is an out-of-distribution (OOD) sample [169], which may trigger unpredictable behaviours and spurious signals.
  • Random baseline: Similarly, adopting a randomly generated skeleton sequence is problematic, as it is highly unlikely to correspond to the distribution of valid skeletal inputs.
  • Particular baseline: Using a random sample from the training set as baseline is also inappropriate, since it will almost certainly lead to a strong activation of the corresponding class.
  • Ambiguous baseline: Inferring a deliberately ambiguous pose sequence to produce low signals is not straightforward, as the class design is tailored to disambiguate almost any human action. Intuitively, most pose sequences can still be assigned, more or less confidently, to one of the classes.
  • Average baseline: A more promising option is to construct an average posture sequence, obtained by averaging a subset of pose sequences from the training set. This approach aims to represent a compromise action across all classes, thereby avoiding the activation of any specific class.

3.6.3 Sensitivity to Feature Removal

Finally, we leverage the remaining action classes, standing and sitting, and generate deletion curves for one instance of each. Specifically, given the attribution scores computed by IG and EG, we progressively remove the same body element from the skeletal sequence by setting its features to zero across the entire temporal dimension, starting from the most important ones defined by the spatial attribution maps, and monitor how the model's confidence in the predicted class decreases. This procedure allows us to determine which attribution method provides more meaningful scores for these particular instances, based on quantitative evidence, while also evaluating the model’s robustness to missing information.

In Figure 45, we present the deletion curves for both methods and calculate the AUC (Area Under the Curve) as a summary statistic. We observe that in both cases, EG yields lower AUC values, indicating that the bones identified as most important are indeed more relevant for the model's prediction. Overall, this trend holds across other instances as well, so even though both methods produce good results, the theoretical advantage of EG becomes apparent in this regard.

Another important aspect, already anticipated during the dataset analysis in Section 2.3 and when examining Table 13, is that the model may be biased toward classes such as sitting when zero values are present in the input features. This bias is also reflected in Figure 45: in both cases, the right and left thighs are identified by both methods as the most important body components for both samples, yet their simultaneous removal causes a larger drop in confidence for the standing instance than for the sitting one. In fact, even when more than $50\%$ of the bones are absent, the model still predicts sitting with a confidence above $0.5$ under both IG and EG removal orders. Although we were already aware of this situation from our previous review, the xAI analysis provided further insight into it.

In general terms, we can conclude that:

  • The model has learned meaningful patterns that are largely consistent with human intuition. These patterns are easier to interpret thanks to the simplicity and denoising nature of the chosen data representation.
  • Both IG and EG provide valuable insights into the model's behavior, although EG appears slightly more reliable.
  • The most important aspects of the body for our problem are those that define the contraction of the human body (thighs and nose–hip connections), together with the arms in certain cases; the head is rarely considered relevant, suggesting that it could be disregarded from the input data representation without significant performance degradation.
  • The model exhibits some biases due to the overrepresentation or underrepresentation of specific conditions for certain classes in the training set, which could be addressed by further improving and expanding the dataset in future iterations.

4.2 Key Components

The DeepStream framework is designed to build pipelines that ingest input from a wide range of sources, including real-time RTSP streams, video files, and USB cameras. To process the data from these sources, it provides a variety of built-in plugins that can be assembled as needed:

  • nvstreammux: combines multiple video input streams into a single batched buffer, synchronizing frames across sources and generating the core metadata structure, which organizes data hierarchically into batches, frames, and objects.
  • nvinfer: executes neural network inference using NVIDIA TensorRT. It supports loading models in the Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) format [176], a widely adopted standard that enables interoperability across different frameworks (e.g., PyTorch, TensorFlow). Models are optimized and compiled into TensorRT engines, which are platform-specific binaries tailored for efficient execution on NVIDIA hardware. These engines can use quantization, that is, the reduction of numerical precision from single-precision floating point (FP32, 32 bits) to half-precision (FP16, 16 bits) or even integer precision (INT8, 8 bits). This process significantly improves inference speed and reduces memory usage, while typically preserving model accuracy [177]. The nvinfer module also allows attaching a custom parser that stores the raw inference outputs into the metadata as required, making them available for successive modules in the pipeline.
  • nvdspreprocess: a highly customizable plugin that performs preprocessing at different levels, including entire frames or selected regions within them. It can handle tasks such as scaling and format conversion, subsequently preparing raw tensors for downstream plugins.

Other modules provide utilities for object tracking (nvtracker), real-time visualization (nvdsosd, nvtiler), and hardware-accelerated video encoding/decoding, allowing the construction of end-to-end systems that process visual information on the fly. Moreover, probe functions can be inserted within these components to interact with the metadata, supporting tasks such as inspection or modification.

4.3 Implementation Design

We implement the complete processing pipeline in DeepStream, following the modular architecture illustrated in Figure 47. The design takes inspiration from [116].

Design of the end-to-end processing pipeline implemented with the NVIDIA DeepStream SDK. Plugins are shown in green, probe functions are highlighted in blue, and inputs/outputs are depicted in red. Numbering corresponds to each of the subsections in Section 2.5.

This architecture takes video frames as input, typically from an RTSP stream, and can produce the same frames overlaid with bounding boxes, skeletons, track IDs, and action labels in real time. It comprises the following components:

  1. Detector (nvinfer): It is implemented as a Primary GPU Inference Engine (PGIE), offering multiple detection for humans at the level of each frame. Frame skipping can be configured if required. The parsing stage integrates the outputs into the metadata, removing low-confidence detections, limiting the maximum number of persons, and suppressing redundant results through non-maximum suppression (NMS). A probe function is registered just after the detector component (i.e., on its source pad) so that detections within predefined regions (such as privacy-sensitive zones, confounding regions, or parts of the frame not of interest) are discarded from the metadata.
  2. Pose Estimator (nvinfer): It acts as a Secondary GPU Inference Engine (SGIE), extracting 2D keypoints and confidence scores from padded RGB crops around each detected person, generated from the bounding boxes of the previous stage. It supports parallel inference over multiple persons with a configurable upper limit, while optionally discarding predictions at a specified rate, or for detections below a minimum size. The model naturally outputs heatmaps, which are then parsed into skeletons relative to the original crop.

    A probe function is attached to the source pad of this plugin to unify bounding boxes and skeletons into a single object for subsequent tracking, while re-projecting keypoints to the original frame coordinates.

  3. Tracker (nvtracker): It assigns a unique ID to each person and maintains it consistently across frames. Both motion-based and appearance-based trackers are supported, with several configuration parameters available. When frame skipping is enabled, bounding boxes are interpolated across skipped frames; however, since pose estimation has already been performed, these interpolated boxes are used only for visualization purposes.
  4. Skeleton Preprocessing Module (nvdspreprocess): Implemented through a custom C++ library, this module maintains a temporal buffer for each of the assigned track IDs. Incoming skeletons are sequentially appended to their corresponding buffer of length $W$, specified by the model configuration, which must be tuned to the input frame rate and pipeline throughput. It then applies visibility thresholding to each keypoint, normalizes coordinates to $[-1,1]$, and transforms every node into a feature representation (e.g., geometric bone), producing a tensor of shape $B \times W \times 17 \times 3$, with $B$ denoting the maximum number of action streams processed in parallel.
  5. Classifier (nvinfer): It serves as another SGIE, performing inference over the tensor generated by the previous module for each tracked identity at the frame being processed. It includes a parser to attach prediction probabilities to the metadata.
  6. Postprocessing and Event Generation (probe function): A probe function is attached to the classifier’s source pad to apply temporal smoothing, averaging class probabilities over a sliding window of the same length $W$ as before. It then selects the most likely class and enforces confidence thresholds to filter out unreliable predictions. According to configurable persistence criteria, the probe generates events that include the cropped sequence, action label, confidence score, and timestamp for each valid prediction. Additionally, it can update the visualization metadata to render fully annotated output frames.

Low-level optimizations were applied to plugins, probe functions, and parse functions to minimize latency. In addition, certain operations of the target models had to be modified to ensure compatibility with ONNX and TensorRT.

4.4 Validation Experiments

In this section, we present a series of ad-hoc experiments designed to provide a partial assessment of the overall pipeline performance. The focus is placed on recall, that is, the ability of the system to be reliably triggered by the actions of interest. Ensuring high recall is critical so that all relevant actions are captured and can, for example, be compiled into reports transmitted to a monitoring center. A high precision is also desirable, although it is mainly relevant in relation to false alarms for the falling class, an aspect that will be addressed later.

Experiments were conducted on real-time video throughput using the selected edge device. A comprehensive analysis of system efficiency, which could be carried out with profiling tools such as [178], is beyond the scope of this work. Our aim here is rather to offer a preliminary view of the system’s behavior and avenues for improvement, as full benchmarking was not among the objectives of this study.

4.4.1 Experimental Setup

The experimental setup is structured around three main aspects: the data used, the system configuration, and the evaluation metrics.
4.4.1.1 Dataset

For these experiments, we leverage a the subset of 20 clips from CCTVActions that we specifically created to simulate sequences of different actions in CCTV scenarios (see Subsection 2.3.4). This set includes multiple chained actions, and although several subjects may appear in the scene, temporal labeling is provided only for a single subject of interest, which is therefore the focus of our evaluation. Despite its limited size, the dataset encompasses a variety of highly challenging situations, particularly for the detector, arising from low resolution, large subject-to-camera distances (ranging from 5 to 50 meters), and severe occlusions staged on purpose. Regarding action recognition, it also reflects certain ambiguities (e.g., a person on the ground leaning back, which can be interpreted either as sitting or lying down), and even atypical frame rates.

4.4.1.2 System Configuration

We will set up the pipeline with a specific configuration, tailored to cover the reflected situations. However, all of these configurations can be easily modified as needed:

  • The detector is the lightweight pretrained version of RT-DETR [119] from ultralytics [179] (RTDETR-L). It is configured in FP16 mode, with a confidence threshold of $0.3$ and a maximum of $16$ detections. The higher limit is chosen to guarantee that, after filtering, at least the most relevant $8$ subjects in the scene are preserved. Privatization layers are defined for each scenario ($7$ in total), covering mainly roads, houses, and vanishing points. No NMS is applied, since RT-DETR was specifically employed to retain occluded individuals. Inference is performed on every frame.
  • The pose estimator is the smallest available version of ViTPose [78], namely ViTPose-S, quantized to FP16. Inference is performed on each detected bounding box, with up to $8$ concurrent inferences and a minimum crop size of $0.1\%$ of the full frame area.
  • The tracker used is NvDCF [180], provided by DeepStream in its optimized version for balancing accuracy and efficiency. It relies on RGB-based discriminative correlation filters for Re-ID.
  • Up to $W=30$ frames from each track are buffered for action recognition, with a keypoint visibility threshold of $0.2$, and geometric bone features.
  • The trained CTR-GCN from Section 3.5 serves as the classifier in FP32 mode, with up to $8$ concurrent action streams, in line with the pose estimator configuration. A confidence threshold of $0.4$ is applied to confirm action predictions, while predictions over buffers shorter than $W=30$ frames are discarded. Post-processing is applied as described.

In Figure 48, we indicate the relative sizes of the main selected components of this configuration.

Relative sizes of the detector, the pose estimator, and the classifier models used in the deployment experiments, expressed in number of parameters (millions).

Modules such as the tracker, probe functions, skeleton preprocessing, and prediction postprocessing also introduce some overhead, although the neural network components dominate the computational cost. The pose estimator is undoubtedly the most expensive module, even though its parameter count is smaller than that of the detector, since it is executed for every detected person (up to a maximum of 8 per frame). Nevertheless, the specific operations of each architecture and the internal optimizations applied by TensorRT for GPU execution are crucial factors that ultimately determine the actual processing time [181]. Therefore, the relative size of the models does not necessarily reflect their impact on the overall system performance, and a thorough efficiency study would be needed to properly address these concerns.

4.4.1.3 Evaluation Metrics
System inferences will be compared to the single-subject ground truth for each clip according to the following considerations:
  • The action labels are expected to appear with a delay of about $W=30$ frames, mainly due to the sliding-window strategy.
  • As the DeepStream system generates labels only up to the end of the stream, the last $30$ ground-truth frames are not considered.
  • Ideally, the system's inference result would be evaluated on a single track corresponding to the subject of interest. However, under challenging conditions, redundant detections, missed detections, and ID switches may occur. A subject can therefore be represented by multiple tracks within a sequence, due to inaccuracies in both the detector and the tracker. We associate predictions with the track that best reflects the subject of interest at each moment. If a detection is redundant, we retain the longest track until it vanishes. If the subject is not detected in a frame, it is considered a missed prediction; the same applies when the subject is detected but has not yet accumulated sufficient evidence (i.e., fewer than $30$ frames linked to the corresponding track).

Following these guidelines, we now define our metric of interest, which resembles mean Average Recall (mAR) but relies on a frame-level recall criterion for thresholding before computing action segment recall. An action segment from the ground truth is considered correctly detected (true positive) for a given class if the system achieves a sufficient proportion of correct frame-level predictions within that segment (accounting for the $30$-frame delay), depending on the applied threshold. From this, we obtain a curve of mAR values per class as a function of the frame-level recall threshold. Averaging across both the class and threshold dimensions then yields a single consolidated mAR score. This idea is consistent with the persistence criteria defined in Subsection 2.5.6.

This approach avoids issues inherent to treating predictions as segments (IoU-based mAR). Although the sliding-window technique mitigates fluctuations, no further smoothing is applied since predictions are expected to be generated on the fly. As a result, phenomena such as flickering or fragmentation could otherwise break these segments and lead to misleading performance assessments.

Additionally, visualizations are provided to gain a qualitative understanding of the system's behavior.

Additionally, we report that not a single frame-level prediction for the falling class was a false positive, underscoring the robustness of the system against spurious detections that could otherwise lead to false alarms.

Final Considerations

To conclude, we summarize the main findings of this work, highlight the objectives achieved, discuss the challenges encountered and potential strategies to overcome them in the future, and close with a reflection on the experience of working as a machine learning engineer.

5.1 Main Conclusions

After revising the state of the art in human action recognition (HAR), with a special focus on real-time systems, we observed that research is largely oriented towards neural networks, given their capacity to generalize complex patterns from data. In this context, we identified several CNN-based designs that address the problem by using inputs based on images or videos. However, considering the set of actions of interest, we noted that RGB data largely introduces unnecessary noise into the process, while also demanding high inference and training costs. In contrast, skeleton-based data discards such redundant information and preserves only the structured kinematic information of human bodies across scenes, providing a more compact, efficient, and interpretable representation that also preserves subject anonymity, a key aspect for privacy. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) are particularly well-suited for processing spatio-temporal data of this kind.

To build an end-to-end HAR system under the specified constraints and leveraging the pose-based approach, we defined a complete pipeline comprising a multi-person detector, a pose estimator applied to the detected crops, a multi-object tracker to generate subject-specific sequences over time, a GCN-based classifier to recognize actions from the skeleton sequences, and prediction enhancement criteria to trigger reliable event alarms or logging. We later integrated this pipeline into DeepStream to enable real-time processing.

Our main focus was the classifier, which required suitable data for training. A review of the literature revealed that no existing dataset fully met our requirements, so we devised a strategy for acquiring and annotating data, resulting in two complementary datasets: Le2iActions , containing examples of the target actions, and CCTVActions , an ad-hoc dataset of real CCTV footage, also designed to address the shortcomings of the former, such as limited diversity in positions, scales, occlusions, frame rates, viewpoints, and action dynamics, as well as class distribution imbalance. In addition, we defined data transformations and augmentations tailored to mitigate data scarcity, reduce unnecessary sources of variability, overcome the well-documented limitations of our datasets, and reflect the pipeline's operational conditions.

The classifier development process involved tuning parameters from pretrained checkpoints. We conducted a progressive study of the key factors affecting training, leading to the following conclusions:

  • As evaluation is focused on assessing the classifier’s performance on the CCTVActions dataset, we also studied how to combine both datasets during training to improve its accuracy and generalization. We observed that different training schemes exhibit distinct convergence patterns, each requiring specific hyperparameter tuning (e.g., learning rate, batch size). We found that joint fine-tuning on both datasets (1-FT) yielded strong results on CCTVActions while also being simple and favoring generalization.
  • Lightweight GCN architectures achieve strong HAR performance when provided with high-quality skeleton sequences. We selected CTR-GCN as our final model, with only $1.4$ million parameters.
  • The way information is encoded in the skeletal graph is crucial both for overall performance and for stable per-class convergence. For our particular set of actions, we found that the proposed geometric bone representation consistently performs better, as it effectively removes negligible sources of variability such as scale or position in the scene, while also highlighting the orientation of kinematic edges. This proved especially beneficial for recognizing static actions.
  • We also argue that applying keypoint masking through Structured Joint Dropout can enable the model to learn more robust patterns.

These last two features proved particularly effective for enabling domain adaptation when relying solely on Le2iActions. Once the final classifier was trained, we evaluated the complete pipeline on the CCTVActions test set, obtaining reliable performance with errors mainly arising from ambiguous situations not well captured by the data representation. This dependable behaviour was further confirmed through an explainability analysis, which showed that the model focuses primarily on logical patterns while also making certain tendencies in our datasets evident.

Finally, we leveraged a highly challenging set of chained actions to validate the complete pipeline running in real time on DeepStream under a selected configuration. The results highlighted the critical role of certain components, particularly the person detector, and also illustrated the impact of elements such as the post-processing strategy. This analysis outlined clear directions for future work.

5.2 Response to Objectives

The initial objectives set out for this work have been successfully achieved:
  • We contextualized the problem of human action recognition through a state-of-the-art review, identifying the most relevant approaches together with their strengths and weaknesses. We effectively motivated the choice of a pose-based approach by highlighting its advantages over alternative methods, particularly in terms of efficiency, interpretability, and privacy. This review was complemented with the theoretical foundations of the neural architectures employed and some historical context.
  • We designed a data acquisition process that leveraged existing public HAR datasets together with specifically collected CCTV data, some of which we simulated ourselves. These datasets covered seven classes of interest, ranging from typical situations to critical or less frequent events such as falls or lying-down scenarios. In particular, CCTVActions was conceived to be sufficiently diverse and tailored to our target deployment scenarios, providing a solid basis for training the classifier.
  • We defined strategies to mitigate the complexity of domain shift under deployment conditions. For this purpose, we employed both deterministic and non-deterministic transformations of the data, including keypoint coordinate normalization for uniformity, smart padding and truncation, Gaussian noise addition, and two techniques specifically introduced in this work that proved particularly effective: geometric bone representation and structured joint dropout. The former reduces noise while emphasizing relevant information, whereas the latter addresses data scarcity to improve robustness.
  • We defined specific metrics to validate our system depending on the stage: classification assessment or whole-pipeline evaluation. These experiments were carried out with an emphasis on methodological rigor. The classification study progressively analyzed several factors in the form of an ablation study, allowing us to determine their individual impact, identify the most suitable configuration, and finally evaluate performance on an independent test set. In particular, the pipeline evaluation was performed on an edge device using DeepStream under real-time conditions, resembling the deployment workflow of use cases in the smart city platform of Innovasur. The scenarios considered were in some cases even more challenging than those encountered in practice, thereby covering conditions beyond typical industrial requirements.
  • Finally, we conducted an explainability analysis to understand the model's behavior. This study confirmed, both qualitatively and quantitatively, that the model focuses on logical patterns while also revealing certain expected biases in our datasets, which could be addressed in future work.

5.3 Limitations and Future Directions

Throughout this work, we critically examined the current state of the system and identified possible directions for its improvement. These considerations are not merely speculative: the project remains open to further refinement, and we intend to continue enhancing it in the future.

Action Set

The set of actions considered aims to cover the most common situations occurring in public spaces, while also including critical events such as falls. However, some situations (for instance, running or cycling) still need to be defined either as separate classes or as subclasses of broader categories (for example, as specific types of walking), and further refinement of the class definitions is required to avoid ambiguity. Further refinement could also incorporate actions involving interaction with objects (e.g., carrying luggage or using a phone), or with other people (e.g., fighting or hugging). Many GCN architectures are already designed to handle inputs involving multiple subjects, which opens the possibility of scaling both the system and the datasets accordingly.

Datasets

Further data collection would help address the limitations identified both in our datasets through exploratory analysis and in the classifier through the xAI study. These include class imbalance, the underrepresentation of certain subactions (e.g., getting up from a lying down position), the bias towards specific viewpoints, which limits generalization to non-overhead CCTV cameras (although less common, these also exist), and keypoint visibility patterns associated with classes such as sitting or lying down.

Data representation

The final evaluation of the classifier revealed inherent limitations of the skeleton-based representation. These include the absence of perspective information in some cases, which could be addressed by incorporating 3D pose estimators, and the lack of a global position reference within the scene for the geometric bone, which is relevant for distinguishing certain actions (e.g., walking vs. standing). Such issues could be mitigated by stacking input channels with additional features (e.g., joints or motion) or by employing ensemble architectures. This multi-stream approach could also be extended to incorporate other modalities, such as RGB data or additional sensors, which may prove beneficial in handling specific edge cases or when expanding the set of actions. Another way of enhancing data representation is by removing unnecessary keypoints that contribute little information, such as some head landmarks (e.g., ears or eyes), or by merging certain keypoints together.

Detector

The detector is a critical component of the pipeline, as it directly determines the quality of the skeleton sequences and, more importantly, their potential absence. Poor detector performance propagates downstream, degrading every subsequent module and ultimately leading to a significant drop in overall system performance, as observed in the pipeline validation experiments. Fortunately, this component is independent and can be replaced with a more powerful option if efficiency constraints permit. We can also explore retraining or fine-tuning on data more representative of deployment scenarios, with particular attention to small-scale pedestrians, occlusions, and especially unusual postures, which fall outside general-purpose detection and may therefore require dedicated adaptation. Ongoing work is focused on this aspect, which we identify as the main bottleneck of the system.

Frame rate management

Currently, we rely on a fixed window size determined by the frame rate of the dataset samples. The classifier is trained without explicitly encoding information about these conditions, under the assumption that it will generalize across different frame rates. Nevertheless, it would be worth exploring strategies for adapting models to specific frame rates, for example by adding interpolated skeletons to the samples according to the target frame rate during training, or alternatively by interpolating inputs according to the classifier's capabilities at inference time. In any case, it would be necessary to ensure that all cameras in the deployment scenario, processed through the same pipeline, operate at a consistent frame rate. In addition, the optimal size of the sliding window could also be empirically investigated.

Postprocessing techniques

Aspects such as confidence filtering, specific persistence criteria, and the use of logical constraints remain to be defined to align with the conditions of the target deployment scenario.

Efficiency

A comprehensive efficiency analysis remains to be carried out, aimed at clearly identifying the most demanding components of the DeepStream pipeline, exploring potential optimizations, and evaluating the trade-off between accuracy and speed. This study should also consider the feasibility of incorporating more action streams in parallel, particularly for handling highly crowded scenes, as well as the impact of supporting multiple input sources on a single device. In addition, the possibility of deploying the system on lighter hardware should be examined, as the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin represents a high-end edge platform that may exceed the requirements of typical deployment scenarios.

Open source

We intend to make certain resources publicly available as part of this research activity, including data samples and demonstrations of the system's functionality, to be uploaded to [182]. However, the code and model checkpoints will not be released due to their proprietary nature.

Appendix A

Data Annotation Workflow

In order to build datasets with intensive annotations, including skeletons and action labels, from raw RGB frames, we follow a semi-automatic approach that avoids the need to manually annotate thousands of keypoints across many different frames. To this end, we leverage pretrained state-of-the-art human detectors, pose estimators, and trackers to automatically annotate the original clips, and then review and correct these annotations using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT) [185].

A.1 Automatic Annotation

The configuration of the automatic annotation pipeline must be flexible enough to handle different types of videos. The clarity of the RGB clips in the Le2iActions dataset (Subsection 2.3.3) allows us to rely on simpler models without extensive parameter tuning. In contrast, the CCTVActions dataset (Subsection 2.3.4) includes people at greater distances and in crowded scenes, requiring more careful model selection and parameter adjustment.

The resulting pipeline is as follows:

  1. We first use a human detector to locate all people in each video frame using bounding boxes. We employ the YOLO v11 [186] model in its most powerful variant (YOLO11x), implemented in the ultralytics library [179], which provides a pretrained checkpoint achieving high mAP on the COCO dataset [72].

    Detection parameters may be adjusted to balance precision and recall. For example, we use a low confidence threshold ($0.02$), a permissive NMS IoU ($0.2$), and cap detections at $10$ per frame to minimize false negatives while controlling noise.

    When trying to detect particular individuals in crowded or occluded scenes, this cap can be increased to reduce the risk of missing true positives; if necessary, we resort to RT-DETR [119] (RTDETR-X implementation in ultralytics), which eliminates NMS and is more robust under heavy overlap.

  2. A tracker is required to link detected bounding boxes across video frames. We employ the BoT-SORT tracker [127], reported to be highly robust for multi-pedestrian tracking by combining both motion and appearance features. It is integrated within the ultralytics framework, and we rely on its optimized default configuration.

    Since tracking is performed offline, we can enforce ID continuity by interpolating bounding box trajectories when a track is temporarily lost, thus accounting for missed detections. Furthermore, track IDs facilitate the manual review stage, allowing entire trajectories of non-relevant subjects to be quickly removed.

  3. A top-down pose estimator is applied over the detected bounding boxes to extract skeletons. We use ViTPose-H [78], a state-of-the-art model for pose estimation that reports strong results on COCO [72], AI Challenger [77], MPII [74], and CrowdPose [76]. The architecture, implemented via the MMPose library [187], outputs 17 2D keypoints in the COCO Keypoints format [73] for each detected person, with a confidence score assigned to every keypoint.

    Instead of retaining these scores in the final annotations, we apply a visibility threshold to derive a visibility flag for each keypoint. This threshold can be adjusted, typically ranging between $0.2$ and $0.4$. If the flag is not triggered, the keypoint coordinates are set to $(0,0)$, effectively discarding the keypoint.

    The pose estimator could be merged with the detector in a single stage using alternatives such as YOLO-Pose [79]. However, we argue that separating detection and pose estimation is advantageous, as it provides greater flexibility in model selection and typically yields better results, especially for distant subjects. In fact, when supplied with a well-localized bounding box, the ViTPose pose estimation model produces remarkably accurate keypoints.

  4. Once this process is completed for an RGB clip, the results are exported into a CVAT for video 1.1 [188] .xml file, which contains the bounding boxes, skeletons, and tracking IDs for each of its frames. Although these annotations are typically stored in .json format, CVAT natively supports only this .xml schema.

We can batch entire directories containing video clips to be processed automatically using the same pipeline configuration. Since annotation time is not a constraint, our priority is accuracy rather than speed. The pipeline is therefore not designed for real-time operation, even though some of its state-of-the-art components are capable of running in such settings.

The pipeline has also been designed with extensibility in mind: it can be adapted to handle different detection categories, alternative skeleton configurations, and multiple input clips presented in various forms.

A.2 Annotation review using CVAT

Videos with their corresponding .xml annotation files can be easily uploaded to a CVAT server instance using the CVAT Python SDK. Each video is defined as a task and assigned to a project that includes two labels: one for person bounding boxes and another for person skeletons, where the available keypoints are specified. In CVAT, bounding boxes and skeletons of the same subject receive different sequential track IDs, but are linked through the concept of grouped shapes.

We will review and correct the annotations frame by frame using the CVAT interface (see Figure 52 for an example). The review criterion is to ensure that every body part of the annotated person(s) is correctly captured, within a certain margin of spatial imprecision. If a body part is occluded such that its relative position with respect to the body cannot be determined, it should be ignored.

CVAT interface showing a frame from a Le2iActions clip, with bounding boxes and skeletons automatically annotated.

These are some of the crucial tasks to ensure high-quality annotations:

  • Delete bounding boxes and skeletons that do not correspond to any person, or to individuals not relevant for annotation. This postprocessing step is straightforward, as deletions can be performed by track ID. For this reason, we emphasized recall in the detection stage for crowded scenes: it is easier to remove false positives than to recover missed detections.
  • If ID switches occur, there is an option to merge tracks, which may involve interpolation of bounding boxes and skeletons. Tracks can also be split when necessary.
  • Correct bounding box or keypoint positions as needed, and set their visibility mask to zero if the original prediction was assigned excessive confidence.
  • Most errors in automatic annotation stem from the detector failing to capture certain people, an inherently challenging task. If a person is missed and their track is lost across frames (thus preventing ID continuity), pose estimation cannot be applied. In such cases, we may either rerun the automatic stage for that video with a different configuration (e.g., lower confidence threshold, alternative model), or manually add bounding boxes and tracks through the CVAT interface, after which only pose estimation needs to be applied.

Once the task is completed, we can download the ground-truth annotations through the CVAT SDK, this time exporting them to .json format for further processing, as it is more standard. As a final step, some checks can be performed to ensure the integrity of the annotations, such as verifying that, while multiple tracks may be annotated per clip, only one is retained for classifier training, and that the number of frames in the original RGB clip matches the annotated frames.

A.3 Clip Segmentation and Structuring

Finally, the annotated clips are trimmed to include only the frames in which the actions of interest occur. This is achieved by specifying timestamps in the form of frame indices, which can be inspected through the CVAT interface, and then applying exact cuts to both the original RGB video (using the precise video manipulation tool ffmpeg [189]) and the corresponding annotation files. The resulting segments may map to to a single action label (for training the classifier), multiple sequential labels for the same subject, or even different labels across multiple subjects. Of course, this trimming process should adhere to the guidelines established in Section 2.1, yet determining the exact frame boundaries is inherently subjective and often requires human judgment.

Although RGB data are not considered in the development of our action recognition system, retaining them is valuable for potential future use. Additionally, joint visualizations of RGB frames together with their associated skeletons provide an effective tool for qualitative analysis. For consistency, videos are stored in .mp4 format using the H.264 codec.

This entire data annotation workflow not only enabled the construction of the datasets, but also provided valuable experience and insights that informed the design of the overall system pipeline.
